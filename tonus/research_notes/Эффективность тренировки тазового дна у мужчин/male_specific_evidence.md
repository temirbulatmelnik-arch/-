## 1. Post-prostatectomy incontinence (PPI): what the Cochrane reviews and key RCTs show, which protocols worked, and whether pre-operative PFMT helps

### Takeaway
After radical prostatectomy (RP), PFMT mainly makes continence come back sooner (clearest at 1–6 months). It does not reliably change continence at 12 months. The pooled Cochrane estimate at 12 months is RR 0.85 (95% CI 0.60–1.22). Programs that worked tended to start before surgery, use biofeedback or supervision, cue the men to contract around the urethra, and use a high dose of contractions that included fast ones (e.g., Milios: 120 contractions/day, standing). Simply adding one-to-one therapy when PFMT leaflets are already available (MAPS) did nothing. **[PATIENTS: post-RP/TURP men]**

### Cited Findings
**Systematic reviews / guidelines**
- Cochrane 2015 (Anderson CA et al., CD001843.pub5): 50 trials, 4,717 men (45 trials after RP, 4 after TURP, 1 after either). Pooled over 8 trials, there was "no evidence that PFMT with or without biofeedback was better than control" for incontinence up to 12 months after RP. At 12 months 57% vs 62% were incontinent, RR 0.85 (95% CI 0.60–1.22); evidence rated **moderate quality**. The review found no evidence of benefit for erectile dysfunction, and only limited evidence for electrical stimulation or magnetic stimulation — [Cochrane abstract PDF](https://cochranelibrary.com/cdsr/doi/10.1002/14651858.CD001843.pub5/pdf/CDSR/CD001843/CD001843_abstract.pdf); [Cochrane full record](https://www.cochranelibrary.com/cdsr/doi/10.1002/14651858.CD001843.pub5/full)
- Cochrane 2023, a newer separate review (Johnson EE, Mamoulakis C, Stoniute A, Omar MI, Sinha S. CD014799.pub2, published 18 Apr 2023, PMID 37070660): combined non-drug, non-surgical treatments "may make little difference" to continence, QoL or adverse events. The evidence is **uncertain** on whether PFMT + biofeedback affects incontinence or QoL. Where pooling was impossible, the authors used a SWiM narrative synthesis — [Cochrane Library](https://www.cochranelibrary.com/cdsr/doi/10.1002/14651858.CD014799.pub2/full); [PMC10112049](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10112049/)
- Hall et al. 2020 (Neurourol Urodyn; systematic review with meta-analyses, 22 RCTs, 15 pooled at 3 months) asked which trial features separate successful from unsuccessful PFMT. At 3 months PFMT beat control in trials that included **pre-operative PFMT (RR 0.76)**, **biofeedback (RR 0.73)** and **instructions to contract around the urethra (RR 0.9)**. The other "success features" were: no PFMT instruction for controls, inclusion of all men, and continence defined as no leakage — [Wiley](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1002/nau.24291); [PubMed 31977112](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31977112/)
- A 2025 systematic review of structured, supervised PFMT given after a correct contraction had been confirmed (J Cancer Surviv): 20 RCTs, 2,444 participants. 13 of 20 (65%) reported significant improvements in continence recovery or symptom severity — [Springer](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s11764-025-01882-6)
- 2023 pairwise meta-analysis (15 RCTs, 2,178 patients): post-operative PFME improved incontinence at 1, 3 and 6 months, with no significant difference at 12 months — [PMC10482454](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC10482454/)
- 2024 network meta-analysis (Frontiers in Oncology; search to Nov 2023; PROSPERO CRD42022331797): biofeedback plus professional therapist guidance ranked best in the first 6 months. Electrical stimulation and biofeedback looked better at 3 months, and combined multimodal therapy was best at 1 year — [PMC10996052](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC10996052/)
- Older DARE-appraised review (2007): evidence was limited and benefits looked short-term. Biofeedback-enhanced training gave higher continence at 1–2 months but not at 3–4 months — [NCBI Bookshelf NBK73486](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK73486/)
- AUA/SUFU guideline "Incontinence after prostate treatment": **pre-operatively**, clinicians *may* offer PFME/PFMT (Conditional Recommendation; Evidence Grade C). **Post-RP**, clinicians *should* offer PFME/PFMT in the immediate post-operative period (Moderate Recommendation; Grade B). Early PFMT shortens time to continence but does not change continence at 12 months — [AUA guideline](https://www.auanet.org/guidelines-and-quality/guidelines/incontinence-after-prostate-treatment); [World Physiotherapy congress summary](https://world.physio/congress-proceeding/interpreting-american-urological-association-incontinence-after-prostate)
- EAU position: a 2019 study abstract states that EAU guidelines "do not recommend pre- and post-operative PFM training for prevention of long term UI". This is a **secondhand** statement that may predate the current EAU edition; I could not reach the EAU text to confirm it — [LSMU abstract](https://lsmu.lt/cris/handle/20.500.12512/98767)

**Landmark RCTs**
- Van Kampen et al. 2000, Lancet 355:98–102 (RCT; 102 men after radical retropubic prostatectomy; 50 PFMT vs 52 placebo therapy). The treatment group got weekly outpatient PFMT + biofeedback (plus electrical stimulation in 7/50), continued for as long as any incontinence lasted, up to 1 year. Primary endpoint: continence at 3 months, reached by **43/48 (88%)** of treated men. Follow-up was 96% — [PEDro record](https://search.pedro.org.au/search-results/record-detail/1554); [EBN commentary](https://ebn.bmj.com/content/3/4/122); Cochrane trial characteristics via [Cochrane references](https://www.cochranelibrary.com/cdsr/doi/10.1002/14651858.CD014799.pub2/references)
- Filocamo et al. 2005, Eur Urol (RCT; 300 consecutive RRP men; 150 structured PFMT started **before discharge, after catheter removal**, vs 150 not formally instructed). Continent at 1 month: **19% (29) vs 8% (12)**. At 6 months: **94.6% (146) vs 65% (97)**, p<0.001. PEDro 5/10 (no concealed allocation, no blinded assessors, no ITT) — [PEDro 13958](https://search.pedro.org.au/search-results/record-detail/13958); [FLORE repository](https://flore.unifi.it/handle/2158/627601)
- Overgård et al. 2008, Eur Urol 54(2):438–48 (RCT, Trondheim; 85 men): weekly 45-min physiotherapist-guided PFMT for up to 1 year vs written/standard PFMT instructions; follow-up at 3, 6 and 12 months. A companion paper found no significant HRQoL difference, even though the guided training improved incontinence versus standard care; 12-month response was 68%. I could not retrieve the exact 12-month continence percentages — [ClinicalTrials.gov NCT00239824](https://clinicaltrials.gov/study/NCT00239824); [UroToday QoL abstract](https://urotoday.com/recent-abstracts/urologic-oncology/prostate-cancer/52422-does-physiotherapist-guided-pelvic-floor-muscle-training-increase-the-quality-of-life-in-patients-after-radical-prostatectomy-a-randomized-clinical-study-abstract.html)
- Glazener et al. 2011, Lancet — MAPS trials (two parallel UK RCTs, ISRCTN87696430). Four one-to-one therapist sessions over 3 months were compared with standard care, which in the UK already included widely available PFMT information. Incontinence at 12 months: after RP **76% vs 77%**; after TURP **65% vs 62%** (both non-significant). The intervention cost £180 and £209 more per man with no QALY gain, and there were no adverse effects. The authors concluded that one-to-one therapy is "unlikely to be effective or cost effective" where PFMT information is already available — [ecancer news](https://ecancer.org/en/news/1866-one-to-one-therapy-to-manage-urinary-incontinence-in-men-following-prostate-surgery-no-more-effective-than-standard-care); [Aberdeen PDF](https://abdn.elsevierpure.com/files/13272349/Glazener_2011.pdf); [ISRCTN](https://www.isrctn.com/ISRCTN87696430)
- Goode et al. 2011, JAMA 305(2):151–9 (RCT; 208 men aged 51–84 with incontinence persisting 1–17 years after RP; 70 behavioural therapy, 70 behavioural + biofeedback + pelvic floor electrical stimulation, 68 control). At 8 weeks, weekly incontinence episodes fell **55% (28→13)** with behavioural therapy, **51% (26→12)** with behaviour-plus, and **24% (25→20)** in controls, who kept diaries and had attention visits. Biofeedback and electrical stimulation added no benefit — [MDedge](https://mdedge.com/content/behavioral-therapy-halves-persistent-postprostatectomy-incontinence); [ClinicalTrials.gov NCT00212264](https://clinicaltrials.gov/study/NCT00212264); [PEDro 28159](https://search.pedro.org.au/search-results/record-detail/28159)
- Milios, Ackland & Green 2019, BMC Urology (RCT; 97 men; retrospectively registered ACTRN12617001400358; PEDro 5/10 — no concealed allocation, no blinding).
  - **Intervention (n=50):** "high-intensity" PFMT starting **5 weeks before RP**: **6 sets/day, each set 10 fast (1 s) + 10 slow (10 s) contractions with equal rest = 120 contractions/day, all in standing**.
  - **Control (n=47):** "usual care" of 3 sets/day × 10 contractions held 10 s with equal rest = 30 contractions/day, done supine, sitting and standing.
  - Both groups continued to 12 weeks post-op.
  - **Results:** controls regained continence more slowly and leaked significantly more (24-h pad weight). The intervention group had longer sustained-contraction scores on real-time ultrasound at every post-op time point.
  - [BMC Urology PDF](https://bmcurol.biomedcentral.com/track/pdf/10.1186/s12894-019-0546-5); [Springer](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s12894-019-0546-5); [ResearchGate summary](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/337300608_Pelvic_floor_muscle_training_in_radical_prostatectomy_A_randomized_controlled_trial_of_the_impacts_on_pelvic_floor_muscle_function_and_urinary_incontinence); [PEDro 59109](https://search.pedro.org.au/search-results/record-detail/59109); [ANZCTR](https://anzctr.org.au/ACTRN12617001400358.aspx)
- Hodges, Stafford et al. 2020, Urol Oncol 38(5):354–371 ("Reconsideration of PFMT to prevent and treat incontinence after radical prostatectomy"; narrative/conceptual review). The paper argues for training tailored to the striated pelvic floor muscles, especially the striated urethral sphincter, with transperineal ultrasound used for assessment and feedback — [ScienceDirect](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S1078143919304946); [Monash](https://research.monash.edu/en/publications/reconsideration-of-pelvic-floor-muscle-training-to-prevent-and-tr/)
- MaTchUP trial (Hodges/Stafford; protocol BMJ Open 2019;9(5):e028288). Planned 363 men randomised to urethral-sphincter-targeted ("personalised") training, conventional training, or no training. Training starts pre-op, with assessment and biofeedback by transperineal ultrasound. Primary outcome: 24-h pad weight at 3 months. **No published results found** — [ResearchGate protocol](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/332886525_Efficacy_of_a_personalised_pelvic_floor_muscle_training_programme_on_urinary_incontinence_after_radical_prostatectomy_MaTchUP_Protocol_for_a_randomised_controlled_trial)
- Stafford et al. 2022 (Neurourol Urodyn) related pre- and post-RP measures of pelvic floor muscle function to early incontinence (observational) — [Wiley](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1002/nau.25034); [PMC9826381](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9826381/)

**Pre-operative PFMT**
- Zhou et al. 2023 (Frontiers in Public Health; meta-analysis of 12 RCTs, 1,365 patients): pre-op PFME gave better continence at **3 months, OR 0.61 (95% CI 0.37–0.98), p=0.04**. Differences at 1, 6 and 12 months were not significant — [PMC10425962](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC10425962/); [Frontiers PDF](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/public-health/articles/10.3389/fpubh.2023.1186067/pdf)
- Wang et al. 2014 (BMC Urology; meta-analysis of 5 RCTs): adding pre-op PFMT did **not** improve UI resolution at ≤3 months, 6 months or 1 year — [PMC4274700](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC4274700/)
- Hall 2020 (above): pre-operative PFMT was a feature of successful trials (RR 0.76 at 3 months) — [Wiley](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1002/nau.24291)

### Inferences
- **[EXTRAPOLATION]** Tonus's dose is roughly equal to or larger than successful post-RP protocols. At level 1 (8 × 3 s holds + 8–20 quick contractions + elevator + long holds, 3 sessions/day) it already exceeds the Milios "usual care" arm (30 contractions/day). Upper levels (12 × 10 s + 20 quick, ×3/day ≈ 96+ contractions/day) approach the Milios high-intensity arm (120/day). Doing more is not obviously better for a healthy man. The trials that worked differ from those that didn't mainly in contraction quality, cueing and feedback, not volume.
- **[EXTRAPOLATION]** Milios ran the whole high-intensity arm **standing**, and the 2019 trial suggests standing is a valid training position. The app's move from sitting to standing at level 5 is consistent with this. Moving standing work earlier, or adding functional standing/walking holds, would also be defensible.
- **[EXTRAPOLATION]** MAPS is relevant to an app. Where PFMT information is already available, adding four therapist sessions did not help. Well-delivered self-directed instruction may therefore capture most of the benefit, as long as the man can actually perform a correct contraction (see §4–5).
- Every PPI result is about restoring continence after surgical damage to the urethral sphincter. It cannot be transferred directly to "toning" a healthy anal sphincter.

### Gaps
- I could not retrieve Van Kampen's placebo-arm or 1-year percentages, or Overgård's 12-month continence rates; full texts were not reachable.
- I could not confirm the current EAU (2025/2026) wording or strength rating on PFMT before or after RP.
- The MaTchUP RCT has no published results that I could find, so the efficacy of ultrasound-guided, urethra-targeted training is still unproven in a large RCT.
- I could not access the GRADE tables of the 2023 Cochrane review.

## 2. Erectile dysfunction (ED) and ejaculatory control: Dorey, Pastore, reviews; effect sizes and protocol parameters

### Takeaway
The best male-specific sexual-function RCT is Dorey 2004 (n=55, men with ED). PFMT + biofeedback + lifestyle advice improved IIEF erectile function by 6.74 points more than lifestyle advice alone at 3 months. After 6 months, about 40% of men had regained normal function and about 35.5% had improved. For lifelong premature ejaculation (PE) the evidence is uncontrolled case series: about 82.5% gained control after 12 weeks of clinic-based rehabilitation, with IELT rising from about 40 s to about 146 s, and the gains waned over 2–3 years. Systematic reviews say PFMT "appears effective" for ED and PE but the studies are low-to-moderate quality and there is no optimal protocol. After prostatectomy, the effect of PFMT on erectile function is inconsistent. **[PATIENTS: men with ED or PE, or post-RP]**

### Cited Findings
- Dorey, Speakman, Feneley, Swinkels, Dunn, Ewings 2004, Br J Gen Pract 54(508):819–825 (RCT with crossover arm; 55 men with ED ≥6 months, median age 59.2, range 22–78).
  - **Arms:** 28 got PFM exercises taught by a physiotherapist, with manometric biofeedback for strength and endurance, plus lifestyle advice. 27 controls got lifestyle advice only.
  - **Outcomes:** assessed at baseline, 3 and 6 months by IIEF-EF, partner rating, anal manometry and digital anal grading.
  - **Results:** at 3 months the intervention group improved **6.74 points more on IIEF-EF than controls (P=0.004)**. Controls then crossed over to the exercises, and improvement continued to 6 months.
  - **Limitations:** PEDro 6/10 with concealed allocation and ITT. No power calculation was possible.
  - [BJGP](https://bjgp.org/content/54/508/819); [UWE repository](https://uwe-repository.worktribe.com/output/1057151/randomised-controlled-trial-of-pelvic-floor-muscle-exercises-and-manometric-biofeedback-for-erectile-dysfunction); [PEDro 10955](https://search.pedro.org.au/search-results/record-detail/10955)
- Dorey et al. 2005, BJU Int (report of the same programme): after 6 months, blinded assessment found **40% regained normal erectile function, 35.5% improved, 24.5% failed to improve** — [BJU Int](https://bjui-journals.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/abs/10.1111/j.1464-410X.2005.05690.x)
- Dorey et al. 2004, Urol Nurs 24(6):490–497 (same RCT, post-micturition dribble outcome). Exercises included a strong **post-void "squeeze out" contraction**, and the authors concluded this treats post-micturition dribble effectively. Percentages were not retrieved — [UWE repository](https://uwe-repository.worktribe.com/output/1056650/pelvic-floor-exercises-for-treating-post-micturition-dribble-in-men-with-erectile-dysfunction-a-randomised-controlled-trial)
- Dorey home-programme details. Secondary sources describe maximal holds of about 10 s with 10 s rest, repeated 8–10 times, plus fast contractions and one set each lying, sitting and standing. **This is not verified against the paper.** One secondary site also quotes a "67% vs 30%" figure that I could not confirm — [pelvicfit.org [secondary]](https://pelvicfit.org/blog/kegel-exercises-for-erectile-dysfunction); [Mend Colorado [secondary]](https://www.mendcolorado.com/physical-therapy-blog/2020/7/20/pelvic-floor-muscle-training-as-a-treatment-for-erectile-dysfunction/)
- Pastore et al. 2014, Ther Adv Urol (uncontrolled prospective series; 40 men with lifelong PE, baseline IELT ≤1 min, mean 39.8 s).
  - **Protocol:** 12 weeks, **3 sessions/week, each with 20 min of physio-kinesiotherapy + 20 min of electrostimulation + 20 min of biofeedback**. The methods were adapted from urinary and faecal incontinence rehabilitation.
  - **Results:** **33/40 (82.5%)** gained control of the ejaculatory reflex, with mean IELT **146.2 s (range 123.6–152.4)**. Only 13 of the 33 responders were assessed at 6 months; they kept a mean IELT of 112.6 s.
  - [PubMed 24883105](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24883105/); [SAGE](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/1756287214523329); [PMC4003840](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4003840/)
- Pastore et al. 2018, Asian J Androl (retrospective long-term series; 154 reviewed, 122 completed). 111 gained control, with mean IELT 161.6 s and PEDT 2.3 at 12 weeks. **64% kept satisfactory control at 24 months and 56% at 36 months** — [PubMed 29974885](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29974885/); [DOAJ](https://doaj.org/article/5eafefc8ecd244c083101636872acb73)
- Myers & Smith 2019, Physiotherapy 105(2):235–243 (systematic review, PROSPERO CRD42016047261). 10 trials (5 ED, 5 PE) in men >18 with no neurological injury or major urological surgery. All ED trials showed improvement or cure. PE results were more mixed, with a wide range of cure rates. Quality was low to moderate and heterogeneity prevented pooling. Protocols varied in therapist contact, co-interventions, duration, frequency and intensity. Conclusion: PFMT "appears effective" for ED and PE, but "no optimal training protocol has been identified" — [UroToday](https://www.urotoday.com/recent-abstracts/men-s-health/erectile-dysfunction/111613-pelvic-floor-muscle-training-improves-erectile-dysfunction-and-premature-ejaculation-a-systematic-review.html); [JCU](https://researchonline.jcu.edu.au/57900/); [Physiotherapy](https://www.physiotherapyjournal.com/article/S0031-9406(19)30007-0/abstract)
- Post-RP erectile function:
  - **Milios et al. 2020** (Sex Med 8(3):414; secondary analysis of the 97-man trial): the 12-week protocol "was not powerful enough to demonstrate conclusive benefits" for erectile function. Early PFMT did reduce the early QoL impact of ED, and EPIC-EF and ultrasound PFM measures improved in both groups — [OUP](https://academic.oup.com/smoa/article/8/3/414/6956708); [PMC7471070](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7471070/)
  - **2026 systematic review** (12 RCTs): effects on erectile function were inconsistent, with 7 trials showing no between-group difference — [UroToday 171701](https://www.urotoday.com/recent-abstracts/men-s-health/erectile-dysfunction/171701-pelvic-floor-muscle-training-for-erectile-function-in-men-undergoing-radical-prostatectomy-a-systematic-review-of-randomized-controlled-trials.html)
  - **2019 meta-analysis of physiotherapy:** no significant long-term difference between PFMT and no treatment for erectile function or climacturia (2 studies). PFMT augmented with biofeedback did improve erectile function after prostatectomy — [UroToday 113283](https://www.urotoday.com/recent-abstracts/men-s-health/erectile-dysfunction/113283-effectiveness-of-physiotherapy-interventions-for-improving-erectile-function-and-climacturia-in-men-after-prostatectomy-a-systematic-review-and-meta-analysis-of-randomized-controlled-trials.html)
  - **Geraerts et al. 2016** (Int J Impot Res; RCT of PFMT for ED and climacturia 1 year after nerve-sparing RP): PEDro 5/10 — [PEDro 49873](https://search.pedro.org.au/search-results/record-detail/49873)
- Cohen, Gonzalez & Goldstein 2016, Sex Med Rev 4:53–62 ("The role of pelvic floor muscles in male sexual dysfunction and pelvic pain") was identified, but its content could not be retrieved — [SMSNA overview](https://smsna.org/news/smsna/pelvic-floor-physical-therapy-for-male-sexual-dysfunctions). A urology review of PFMT in males covers SUI, OAB, post-void dribbling, ED, ejaculatory problems including PE, and pelvic pain from levator spasm — [Urology News review](https://www.urologynews.uk.com/reviews/journal-reviews/post/pfmt-in-males)

### Inferences
- **[EXTRAPOLATION]** Dorey found the main gain by 3 months and further gains to 6 months. That timeline suits the app's 8-level structure if the levels take about 3–6 months in total. A healthy man with normal erections has a "ceiling" and should not expect measurable erectile gains; the sexual-function evidence is in men with ED or PE.
- **[EXTRAPOLATION]** The PE benefit came from 12 weeks of clinic sessions combining electrostimulation and biofeedback, and part of it was lost by 24–36 months. Home voluntary training alone has not been shown to reproduce it, and continued (maintenance) training is plausibly needed.
- **[EXTRAPOLATION]** A post-void "squeeze-out" contraction is a cheap, evidence-based add-on (Dorey) that the app could offer as an optional habit.

### Gaps
- I could not verify the exact Dorey home protocol (the brief's "3×/day, 18 maximal contractions lying, sitting, standing") against the BJGP or BJU Int full text. It may be consistent with the secondary descriptions (sets in three positions) but remains unconfirmed.
- I found no RCT of PFMT for PE with a sham or control arm; Pastore's work is uncontrolled.
- I found no study of PFMT for sexual function in sexually healthy men.
- I could not retrieve the content of Rosenbaum 2007 or Siegel 2014 ("Pelvic floor muscle training in males: practical applications", Urology).

## 3. Faecal/flatus incontinence (FI) in men and anal sphincter weakness; anal manometry norms by sex

### Takeaway
Men have higher anal squeeze pressures than women in most normative studies; resting pressure differences are inconsistent. Male-specific trials of anal-sphincter training are almost absent. The FI evidence (Cochrane: 21 trials, 1,525 participants; Norton 2003) is mostly in women and shows that advice, exercises and biofeedback all improve symptoms and pressures, with biofeedback adding little. After rectal cancer surgery (mixed-sex, often male-majority cohorts), PFMT speeds short-term recovery from LARS, but the benefit fades by about 12 months. **[PATIENTS: FI, LARS; largely mixed-sex or female]**

### Cited Findings
**Manometry norms by sex [HEALTHY]**
- Korean healthy-volunteer HR-ARM study: men had higher maximum squeeze pressure (**211.0 ± 50.7 vs 157.5 ± 42.5 mmHg**) and higher resting anal pressure (**71.6 ± 18.1 vs 58.8 ± 20.5 mmHg**) than women — [Neurogastroenterol Motil via Ovid](https://www.ovid.com/journals/negmot/fulltext/10.1111/nmo.12297~normal-values-for-high-resolution-anorectal)
- Vietnamese young adults (15 men, 15 women): all anal pressures differed significantly by sex. Overall means: resting 65.5, squeeze 168.0, cough 125.9, strain 84.2 mmHg — [BMC Gastroenterol / PMC8281378](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC8281378/)
- Indian healthy volunteers: median resting (88 vs 94 mmHg) and squeeze (165 vs 147 mmHg) did **not** differ significantly by sex. A Brazilian study found higher resting pressure in women (79.8 vs 72.2 mmHg) but higher squeeze in men (229.5 vs 170.7 mmHg) — [J Neurogastroenterol Motil](https://www.jnmjournal.org/journal/view.html?uid=1776&vmd=Full); [PubMed 28789489](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28789489)
- Review summary: squeeze pressures are lower in women than men and in older than younger people, so norms must be stratified by age and sex and are equipment- and lab-specific — [ScienceDirect Topics: anal manometry](https://www.sciencedirect.com/topics/medicine-and-dentistry/anal-manometry); [JNM how-to](https://www.jnmjournal.org/journal/view.html?doi=10.5056%2Fjnm15168)

**Sphincter physiology**
- Resting anal canal pressure comes mostly from the internal anal sphincter (IAS; smooth muscle, involuntary). The rise during voluntary squeeze comes from the striated external anal sphincter (EAS) — [PMC review](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC1411081); [Western Sydney Univ GI motility](https://www.westernsydney.edu.au/gimotility/gi_disorders/anorectal_manometry)
- In a 1976 Gut study, maximal resting pressure, "mainly due to internal sphincter activity", was lower in incontinent (31 mmHg) than continent patients (67 mmHg), while the squeeze increment did not differ significantly — [Gut 17(2):147](https://gut.bmj.com/content/17/2/147)
- A breakdown of resting tone as about 55% IAS, 15% haemorrhoidal plexus and 30% EAS appears only in a patent document (**weak source; not peer-reviewed**) — [USPTO patent 8439819](https://image-ppubs.uspto.gov/dirsearch-public/print/downloadPdf/8439819)
- When puborectalis contracts, it pulls the anorectal junction forward and sharpens the anorectal angle — [ScienceDirect Topics: puborectalis](https://www.sciencedirect.com/topics/veterinary-science-and-veterinary-medicine/puborectalis-muscle)

**Training trials in FI (mostly women / mixed)**
- Norton & Cody 2012, Cochrane CD002111.pub3: 21 trials, 1,525 participants, covering biofeedback and/or sphincter exercises. Standards of treatment are still lacking and "the magnitude of alleged benefits has yet to be established" — [Cochrane](https://cochranelibrary.com/cdsr/doi/10.1002/14651858.CD002111.pub2/information); [PubMed 22786479](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/22786479/)
- Norton et al. 2003, Gastroenterology 125(5):1320–9 (RCT; 171 FI patients in 4 arms: advice alone; advice + sphincter exercises; computer-assisted biofeedback; biofeedback + home device). All groups improved **resting, squeeze and sustained squeeze pressures (all P<0.002)**, largely maintained at 1 year. 53% vs 54% improved (biofeedback vs advice only), so neither exercises nor biofeedback beat advice and education — [PubMed 14598248](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/14598248/); [Relias summary](https://reliasmedia.com/articles/24400-randomized-controlled-trial-of-biofeedback-for-fecal-incontinence)
- Meta-analysis of physiotherapy for FI after colorectal surgery: PFMT + biofeedback outperformed PFMT alone for anal resting pressure, squeeze pressure and rectal resting pressure, and both beat usual care. Results were not broken out by sex — [PMC10787910](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC10787910/)
- Asnong et al. 2022, Ann Surg 276(5):761–768 (multicentre single-blind RCT; 104 patients; PFMT n=50 vs none n=54, starting 1 month after TME surgery or stoma closure). The primary endpoint was improvement in LARS category at 4 months. A BJS Academy summary reports improvement at 6 months of **47.8% vs 21.3% (P=0.0091)**, lost by 1 year. Authors' conclusion: faster recovery up to 6 months **[secondary numbers]** — [BJS Academy](https://www.bjsacademy.com/the-role-of-pelvic-floor-muscle-training-on-low-anterior-resection-syndrome-a-multicenter-randomized-controlled-trial); [UHasselt](https://documentserver.uhasselt.be/handle/1942/38830)
- Argentine HTA: biofeedback plus pelvic floor exercises "might provide considerable benefits" for FI symptom remission in men and women in the short term (moderate-quality evidence) — [INAHTA](https://database.inahta.org/article/20001)

### Inferences
- **[EXTRAPOLATION]** A healthy young man starts near the top of the squeeze-pressure range (often ~170–230 mmHg). Gains from voluntary training will be relative to a high baseline, and a home app cannot measure them without a sensor.
- **[EXTRAPOLATION]** Voluntary training targets the EAS and puborectalis, which drive squeeze pressure, squeeze endurance and urge control. Resting tone is mostly the involuntary IAS. Norton 2003 did see resting pressure rise in all arms (advice included), but that was in FI patients with deficits and cannot be attributed to the exercises.
- The FI/LARS pattern (faster early recovery, benefit fading by 12 months, biofeedback adding little over good instruction) mirrors the post-RP pattern.

### Gaps
- I found no male-only RCT of sphincter training for FI, including after haemorrhoidectomy or fistulotomy.
- I could not retrieve Visser et al. 2014 (Ann Coloproctol systematic review of pelvic floor rehabilitation after LAR) or Bols et al. (PhysioFIT RCT).
- I found no peer-reviewed percentage breakdown of resting pressure by sex.

## 4. Healthy men and male athletes: does PFMT improve strength, squeeze pressure, sexual function or continence? How common is male pelvic floor dysfunction in athletes?

### Takeaway
I found **no RCT of PFMT in healthy, asymptomatic men or male athletes** measuring strength, squeeze pressure, sexual function or continence. Two things are documented in healthy men: young asymptomatic men cannot be assumed to contract correctly, and the cue chosen strongly changes whether the contraction lifts. Male athletes report LUTS or urinary incontinence at roughly 4–19%, rising with training volume, but the evidence base is small. **[HEALTHY: cueing and prevalence only; training-effect evidence must be extrapolated from patients]**

### Cited Findings
- Scott, Osmotherly & Chiarelli 2013 (Univ. of Newcastle; 52 healthy men): "no assumptions should be made that an ability to contract those muscles is present or effective in young, asymptomatic men" — [NOVA Newcastle](https://nova.newcastle.edu.au/vital/access/%20/manager/Repository/uon:13305)
- Ben Ami, Feldman & Dar 2022, IJERPH 19(19):12031 (35 healthy male physiotherapy students, mean age 25.9; transabdominal ultrasound of bladder-base displacement; 6 verbal cues). With **"squeeze your anus", "shorten the penis" and "elevate the scrotum", over 91%** produced a correct cranial (upward) bladder-base displacement. With **"draw in"** (breathing + PFM + transversus abdominis) only **25%** did, and it gave the lowest endurance. The authors advise avoiding "draw in" and the generic "squeeze your PFM" — [PMC9566287](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC9566287/); [MDPI](https://www.mdpi.com/1660-4601/19/19/12031)
- Scoping review 2025 (J Sci Med Sport; 10 studies of male athletes): 6 studies reported LUTS including UI at **3.8%–18.8%**. Cross-sectional data showed a **dose-dependent relationship between weekly MET-minutes and LUTS prevalence**, and athletic males "may experience higher rates of LUTS than the general population" — [UroToday 164501](https://www.urotoday.com/recent-abstracts/pelvic-health-reconstruction/stress-incontinence/164501-prevalence-of-pelvic-floor-dysfunction-in-male-athletes-and-its-dose-dependency-in-high-intensity-exercise-a-scoping-review.html); [Univ. Canberra](https://researchprofiles.canberra.edu.au/en/publications/prevalence-of-pelvic-floor-dysfunction-in-male-athletes-and-its-d/)
- Scoping review 2021 (Neurourol Urodyn; male and female athletes): only 12 studies explored PFD in men. Major gaps include male-focused studies and other dysfunctions (anal incontinence, prolapse, pelvic pain) — [PubMed 33137211](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33137211/); [ICS 2020 abstract](https://ics.org/2020/abstract/360)
- Older, non-systematic claims of ">50% UI in elite athletes" come from mixed-sex samples and should not be applied to men — [POGP journal](https://thepogp.co.uk/journal/17/acpwh_journal_112_spring_2013/322)
- The 2005 review of RCTs in men (Dorey) covers post-prostatectomy continence, post-micturition dribble and erectile function, all **clinical populations** — [PubMed 16301945](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16301945/)

### Inferences
- **[EXTRAPOLATION]** For a healthy athletic man, the best-supported benefits are: (a) learning a correct, isolated and lifting contraction, which cannot be taken for granted; (b) possibly preventing exertional LUTS, which become more common with high training loads; and (c) better voluntary control and endurance. Claims of measurable "strength gains" in healthy men are extrapolated from patient and female data.
- **[EXTRAPOLATION]** Training-volume-related LUTS in athletes means heavy lifting, running or jumping work could be added at later levels: brace-before-load ("the knack") and standing or functional holds.

### Gaps
- No RCT or controlled study of PFMT in healthy men or male athletes with outcomes such as squeeze pressure, ultrasound displacement over time, sexual function or continence.
- No male-athlete prevalence data on anal or flatus incontinence or pelvic pain.

## 5. Correct contraction cues for men (Stafford et al.; transperineal ultrasound): which muscles each cue activates; implications for a sphincter-focused program

### Takeaway
In healthy men, "shorten the penis" and "stop the flow of urine" produced the greatest mid-urethral displacement and striated urethral sphincter (SUS) activity. Anal cues ("tighten around the anus", "hold in wind") bias the contraction to the posterior pelvic floor: the EAS and puborectalis. Interior or global cues ("draw in", "squeeze your pelvic floor") perform poorly. For urinary continence, trials that cued the urethra did better (Hall 2020). For a sphincter-focused goal, Tonus's anal cue is appropriate, but it should be paired with an anterior cue so the whole floor is trained. **[HEALTHY for cue mechanics; PATIENTS for clinical relevance]**

### Cited Findings
- Stafford, Ashton-Miller, Constantinou, Coughlin, Lutton & Hodges 2016, Neurourol Urodyn 35(4):457–463, DOI 10.1002/nau.22745, published online 2015 ("Pattern of activation of pelvic floor muscles in men differs with verbal instructions").
  - **Design:** 15 healthy men (ages 28–44, no prior PFMT) did sub-maximal contractions to four cues: "tighten around the anus", "elevate the bladder", "shorten the penis", "stop the flow of urine". Fine-wire EMG of **puborectalis** and **bulbocavernosus** was recorded in 3 men, and transurethral EMG of the **SUS**.
  - **Results [secondary]:** summaries report the **greatest dorsal displacement of the mid-urethra and SUS activity with "shorten the penis"**. They recommend "shorten the penis" and "stop the flow of urine" for optimal SUS activation, and note that men do not all respond the same way to each cue.
  - [Wiley](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1002/nau.22745); [Herman & Wallace [secondary]](https://hermanwallace.com/blog/verbal-instructions-and-pelvic-floor-contractions-in-men); [Mend Colorado [secondary]](https://www.mendcolorado.com/physical-therapy-blog/2020/9/3/what-are-the-best-cues-to-reduce-urinary-incontinence-in-men/)
- Stafford et al. 2012, J Urol 188(4):1224–30 ("Novel insight into the dynamics of male pelvic floor contractions through transperineal ultrasound imaging") established transperineal ultrasound landmarks in men (detailed results not retrieved). A related ICS abstract reports that the penile bulb moves more during contraction in continent than in incontinent post-prostatectomy men — [UQ profile](https://shrs.uq.edu.au/node/165); [ICS abstract](https://www.ics.org/Abstracts/Publish/180/000139.pdf)
- Stafford et al. (validation study, PMC4671687; fine-wire EMG in 3 healthy men): displacement of transperineal ultrasound landmarks gives "meaningful measures of activation of individual pelvic floor muscles in men during voluntary contractions". **Raised intra-abdominal pressure prevented puborectalis shortening**, weakening the EMG–displacement relationships — [PMC4671687](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC4671687/)
- Physiotherapy management review (J Physiother 2021): wording such as "stop the urine flow" or "shorten the penis" focuses men on the urethral sphincter. Cues aimed at stopping wind focus attention on the posterior pelvic floor and EAS — [ScienceDirect](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S183695532100014X)
- Hall 2020 meta-analysis: trials that instructed contraction around the urethra had better 3-month continence (RR 0.9) — [Wiley](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1002/nau.24291)
- Ben Ami 2022 (healthy young men): "squeeze your anus", "shorten the penis" and "elevate the scrotum" each produced a correct lift in >91%. "Draw in" worked in 25%; avoid "draw in" and "squeeze your PFM" — [PMC9566287](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC9566287/)
- 2024 electrical-stimulation study in 10 men: puborectalis contraction was evoked in fewer men than sphincter contraction with a perineal electrode montage. Puborectalis appears harder to isolate than the sphincters — [ScienceDirect](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S2772973724001486)
- Clinical cue frameworks divide the male floor into front, middle and back. "Shorten your penis" targets the front and middle; "tighten around the anus" targets the back, which relates to faecal continence — [Diane Lee Physio [secondary]](https://dianeleephysio.com/education/movember-urinary-continence-muscle-training-for-the-male-pelvic-floor/); [Physiopedia](https://www.physio-pedia.com/The_Male_Pelvic_Floor)

### Inferences
- **[EXTRAPOLATION]** Tonus's cue ("hold in wind, squeeze the ring around the anus and lift it up and in") is well matched to the user's goal: it biases the contraction to the EAS and, through "lift", to the puborectalis/levator ani. Ben Ami found "squeeze your anus" produced a correct lift in >91% of healthy young men.
- **[EXTRAPOLATION]** A purely anal cue may under-recruit the SUS and bulbocavernosus. These muscles matter for continence under load, post-void dribble and erection rigidity (§8). A rotating "whole floor" cue would train the full floor: back ("hold wind"), middle ("lift up and in"), front ("shorten the penis / stop the stream"). It could be one quick-contraction set per session using "shorten the penis".
- **[EXTRAPOLATION]** Avoid "draw in" or abdominal-hollowing phrasing during holds. Warn against breath-holding and bearing down, since raised intra-abdominal pressure blocks puborectalis shortening (Stafford validation study). Good checks are: breathing continues, the buttocks and thighs stay relaxed, and the penis and scrotum lift slightly (a home self-check by mirror or touch).
- **[EXTRAPOLATION]** "Stop the flow of urine" is fine as a *mental image*. It should not become a routine practice of interrupting voiding; that is a common clinical caution, but I did not find a male-specific source for it here.

### Gaps
- I could not retrieve per-cue EMG and displacement numbers from Stafford 2016, including which cue maximised puborectalis/anorectal-junction displacement and bulbocavernosus EMG.
- I found no study directly comparing "hold in wind" with "squeeze the anus" in men.

## 6. Male chronic pelvic pain syndrome (CP/CPPS) and an overactive pelvic floor: when strengthening is contraindicated, the role of relaxation (Stanford protocol), and warning signs

### Takeaway
In men with CP/CPPS the pelvic floor is often over-active or tender, and the documented treatments emphasise relaxation, myofascial release and down-training, not strengthening. The Stanford protocol (Anderson/Wise; myofascial trigger point release + paradoxical relaxation) reported moderate or marked improvement in 72% of 138 refractory men, in an uncontrolled study. The overall CP/CPPS physiotherapy evidence is low quality. I found no trial that directly tests whether Kegel strengthening worsens CPPS; the contraindication is clinical reasoning, not trial evidence. **[PATIENTS: CP/CPPS]**

### Cited Findings
- Anderson, Wise, Sawyer, Chan 2005, J Urol 174(1):155–60 ("Integration of myofascial trigger point release and paradoxical relaxation training treatment of chronic pelvic pain in men"; uncontrolled cohort). 138 men with CP/CPPS refractory to conventional therapy were treated by a urologist, physiotherapist and psychologist for at least 1 month. The protocol rests on the hypothesis that CP symptoms are perpetuated by abnormal pelvic floor contraction — [PubMed 15947608](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/15947608/)
- Reported outcomes **[secondary — Urology Times]**: **72%** reported moderate-to-marked global improvement, with median NIH-CPSI total-score drops of about 24–46%. Among improvers, pain and urinary scores fell 69% and 80%. About **21% worsened** — [Urology Times](https://www.urologytimes.com/view/two-pronged-approach-relieves-refractory-cpps-pain-0)
- Pelvic floor PT for pelvic floor hypertonicity (systematic review; Sex Med Rev): PFPT "seems to be efficacious" in CP/CPPS, vulvodynia and dyspareunia, but most studies have a high risk of bias — [PubMed 34127429](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34127429/)
- 2025 systematic review of physiotherapy for male CP/CPPS (13 studies, 600 patients): studies are not of sufficient quality to support specific recommendations — [Termedia / Medical Studies](https://www.termedia.pl/doi/10.5114/ms.2025.152432)
- Biofeedback study in 33 men with CP/CPPS: patients learned to **relax and contract** the pelvic floor. Pelvic floor tone fell after treatment, but durability was not studied — [BVS record mdl-15826751](https://busqueda.bvsalud.org/portal/resource/es/mdl-15826751)
- Men with chronic prostatitis reportedly have higher resting pelvic floor tension and less ability to relax after a contraction (Moheb et al., cited by a clinic blog) **[secondary]** — [Mend Colorado [secondary]](https://www.mendcolorado.com/physical-therapy-blog/2022/10/6/physical-therapy-as-a-treatment-for-chronic-prostatitis/)
- EAU Guidelines on Chronic Pelvic Pain recommend pelvic floor physiotherapy; a Dutch survey found low physiotherapist adherence to EAU criteria. The exact EAU wording on overactivity was not retrieved — [Guideline Central summary](https://www.guidelinecentral.com/guideline/4543287/); [Leiden thesis](https://scholarlypublications.universiteitleiden.nl/access/item%3A3655493/download)
- Dyssynergia is defined as paradoxical contraction or inability to relax the pelvic floor when straining or bearing down — [Transl Androl Urol](https://tau.amegroups.org/article/view/16851/html)

### Inferences
- **[EXTRAPOLATION]** For the app: a healthy athletic man pursuing a "tight" sphincter is exactly the profile at risk of over-training into a guarded, non-relaxing floor. A relaxation or reverse-Kegel block in every session is well justified (Tonus already has one), and the release after each hold matters as much as the hold.
- **[EXTRAPOLATION — screening and red flags]** The app should pause strengthening and recommend assessment by a pelvic health physiotherapist or urologist if the user reports any of:
  - perineal, testicular, penile-tip, rectal or suprapubic pain;
  - pain during or after ejaculation;
  - pain when sitting;
  - hesitant or slow urination, or incomplete emptying;
  - new constipation, straining, or a feeling of incomplete evacuation;
  - pain or spasm during or after training;
  - inability to feel the muscle "let go".

  These signs are drawn from the CP/CPPS and dyssynergia literature above.
- **[EXTRAPOLATION]** Also stop and refer for: blood in the stool or urine, new faecal or urinary incontinence, or rectal bleeding or a lump. These need medical assessment, not training.

### Gaps
- I found no RCT showing that Kegel strengthening worsens male CPPS. "Contraindication" rests on mechanism and clinical consensus.
- I could not retrieve the 2018 Anderson/Wise/Nathanson meta-analysis (Urology) or the Stanford 6-day intensive-protocol results.

## 7. Program duration, timeline of improvement, maintenance and adherence in men; app-based programs for men

### Takeaway
In men, improvements appear within the first 1–3 months: post-RP continence by 1 month (19% vs 8%) and 3 months (88% continent with PFMT), and ED gains by 3 months. Gains keep accruing to 6 months, but PFMT's advantage over control usually disappears by 12 months after RP, and PE benefits wane over 2–3 years. Two app or mHealth RCTs disagree: a 2026 app RCT (n=62) reported 74.2% vs 21.4% continence at 90 days, while a 2022 US mHealth RCT (n=245) found no benefit over mHealth education. Engagement with men's apps falls off quickly (about 41% of users responded at 1 month, about 34% at 3 months). **[PATIENTS]**

### Cited Findings
- Dorey 2004 (ED): improvement by 3 months (IIEF-EF +6.74 vs control) and further improvement to 6 months — [BJGP](https://bjgp.org/content/54/508/819); [BJU Int 2005](https://bjui-journals.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/abs/10.1111/j.1464-410X.2005.05690.x)
- Filocamo 2005 (post-RRP): continent 19% vs 8% at 1 month, 94.6% vs 65% at 6 months — [PEDro](https://search.pedro.org.au/search-results/record-detail/13958)
- Van Kampen 2000: 88% (43/48) continent at 3 months with PFMT, treated weekly until dry, up to 1 year — [PEDro](https://search.pedro.org.au/search-results/record-detail/1554)
- Goode 2011: a 55% reduction in leakage episodes after 8 weeks of behavioural therapy, even 1–17 years after RP — [MDedge](https://mdedge.com/content/behavioral-therapy-halves-persistent-postprostatectomy-incontinence)
- Pooled analyses: benefit at 1–6 months, no difference at 12 months — [PMC10482454](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC10482454/); Cochrane RR 0.85 at 12 months — [Cochrane](https://cochranelibrary.com/cdsr/doi/10.1002/14651858.CD001843.pub5/pdf/CDSR/CD001843/CD001843_abstract.pdf)
- Pastore PE: 64% kept control at 24 months and 56% at 36 months after a 12-week course — [PubMed 29974885](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29974885/)
- PELVINTENSE (multicentre RCT; Cancers 2026; doi 10.3390/cancers18091333). Modular app-based PFMT vs standard physiotherapist-guided PFMT, both from 3 weeks pre-op to 90 days post-op, in 62 pre-operatively continent men (enrolled Sept 2022–Sept 2024). Continence (ICIQ-SF Q1 = 0) at 90 days was **74.2% vs 21.4%**, **OR 13.80 (95% CI 3.22–59.12)**. The trial is small with a very wide CI, and the adherence results were not retrieved — [PubMed 42122130](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42122130/); [PMC13163010](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13163010/); [UroToday](https://www.urotoday.com/recent-abstracts/pelvic-health-reconstruction/stress-incontinence/169018-efficacy-of-a-modular-app-based-pelvic-floor-muscle-training-program-for-postoperative-continence-recovery-after-radical-prostatectomy-a-multi-center-randomized-controlled-trial-pelvintense-study.html)
- Goode et al. 2022, J Urol 208(2):379–387 (3-site RCT, 2014–2019, NCT01960998). 245 men aged 42–78 were randomised to an mHealth perioperative behavioural program (1–4 weeks pre-op + 8 weeks post-op) or mHealth general prostate cancer education. There was **no difference in time to continence** (Kaplan–Meier), and ICIQ at 6 months was **7.1 vs 7.0 (p=0.7)** — [UroToday 136445](https://www.urotoday.com/recent-abstracts/pelvic-health-reconstruction/post-prostatectomy-incontinence/136445-perioperative-mobile-telehealth-program-for-post-prostatectomy-incontinence-a-randomized-clinical-trial.html); [ClinicalTrials.gov NCT01960998](https://clinicaltrials.gov/study/NCT01960998)
- Al-Zaidi et al. 2022, BJUI Compass (Swedish "Tät-m" men's app; prospective cohort). 388 of 3,043 users met the inclusion criteria. **41.0% answered at 1 month and 33.7% at 3 months.** Of 3-month responders, 96.9% said the app helped "a lot" or "somewhat". Confidence in contracting correctly rose from **39.7% to 74.0%** at 1 month (self-report, anonymous, response bias likely) — [PMC9766858](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC9766858/); [App Store listing](https://apps.apple.com/app/id1370860368); [ICS 2019 abstract](https://www.ics.org/2019/abstract/548)
- MAPS: four one-to-one sessions over 3 months were no better than standard written information — [ecancer](https://ecancer.org/en/news/1866-one-to-one-therapy-to-manage-urinary-incontinence-in-men-following-prostate-surgery-no-more-effective-than-standard-care)

### Inferences
- **[EXTRAPOLATION]** A realistic promise for Tonus users: noticeable control and endurance changes within about 4–8 weeks, more by about 3 months, and a plateau by about 6 months. After that the program should switch to a lower-frequency maintenance phase (e.g., one session a day or a few times a week). That is extrapolated from muscle-training principles and the waning seen in PE and post-RP follow-ups; it is not a tested male maintenance protocol.
- **[EXTRAPOLATION]** Tonus progresses after 9–21 sessions per level at 3 sessions a day, so 8 levels take roughly 3–7 weeks of daily use. That is faster than the 3–6-month time course of trial benefits. Requiring calendar time per level, not just a session count, would better match the physiology.
- **[EXTRAPOLATION]** The steep drop-off in engagement with a men's PFMT app (to about a third by 3 months) argues for adherence features: reminders, short sessions, streaks, and a quick "check your contraction" self-test.

### Gaps
- I could not retrieve PELVINTENSE adherence data or app content details.
- No male-specific maintenance-dose study was found.
- I found no app RCT in healthy men.

## 8. Can the anal opening become "tighter" with training? What actually changes (strength, tone/resting pressure, endurance, control), what doesn't (anatomy), and which male muscles matter

### Takeaway
Voluntary training acts on striated muscle: the EAS, puborectalis/levator ani, bulbospongiosus and ischiocavernosus. It can improve squeeze pressure, sustained squeeze (endurance), timing and coordination, and urge or flatus control. Resting "tone" is mostly the involuntary internal anal sphincter, so a lasting rise in resting tightness is not an established training effect in healthy men. The anal canal's size and structure do not change. I found no study showing EAS hypertrophy on imaging after training; the only link is indirect, in that sphincter bulk correlates with squeeze pressure. **[Mixed: physiology + patient data; no healthy-male training data]**

### Cited Findings
- Resting anal pressure is mostly IAS; the voluntary squeeze increment is EAS — [PMC review](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC1411081); [Gut 1976](https://gut.bmj.com/content/17/2/147)
- The IAS is normally in continuous contraction to prevent leakage of faeces or gas (tertiary source with a citation-needed tag) — [Wikipedia: Internal anal sphincter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Internal_anal_sphincter)
- In FI patients, all arms of Norton 2003, including advice only, improved resting, squeeze and sustained-squeeze pressures (P<0.002), largely kept at 1 year. Pressure gains therefore occurred without exercise-specific superiority — [PubMed 14598248](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/14598248/)
- After colorectal surgery, PFMT ± biofeedback improved anal resting and squeeze pressures versus usual care — [PMC10787910](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC10787910/)
- Imaging: in 8 healthy volunteers, EAS dorsal thickness was **6.35 ± 1.07 mm on ultrasound vs 3.99 ± 0.99 mm on MRI**, and only the IAS correlated between methods. Any before-and-after hypertrophy study must therefore use one modality. Sphincter muscle area on MRI correlated strongly with squeeze pressure (indirect evidence linking bulk to function) — [Springer (Dis Colon Rectum)](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/BF02050142); [PMC3949029](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3949029)
- Bulbocavernosus (bulbospongiosus): voluntary or evoked contraction **raised intracavernous pressure and reduced erectile inflow**, showing its role in penile rigidity (human study) — [Karger, Eur Urol 18(1):45](https://karger.com/eur/article-abstract/18/1/45/130028/Role-of-the-Bulbocavernosus-Muscles-on-the)
- Ischiocavernosus compresses the crura and can drive suprasystolic intracavernous pressure; neurostimulation work reported **120–300 mmHg** (animal data). One uncontrolled study of 122 men with ED suggested a role for ischiocavernosus atrophy and benefit from rehabilitation — [HAL Lyon 1](https://hal-univ-lyon1.archives-ouvertes.fr/hal-02046825)
- The pudendal nerve supplies the urethral and anal sphincters, bulbospongiosus and ischiocavernosus — [Herman & Wallace [secondary]](https://hermanwallace.com/blog/pelvic-muscle-rehabilitation-and-mens-sexual-health)
- Puborectalis sharpens the anorectal angle when it contracts. It is harder to isolate than the sphincters, and its shortening is blocked by raised intra-abdominal pressure — [ScienceDirect Topics](https://www.sciencedirect.com/topics/veterinary-science-and-veterinary-medicine/puborectalis-muscle); [PMC4671687](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC4671687/); [ScienceDirect 2024](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S2772973724001486)
- In continent post-prostatectomy men, the penile bulb displaces more during contraction than in incontinent men, which ties bulbospongiosus and anterior-floor activity to function — [ICS abstract](https://www.ics.org/Abstracts/Publish/180/000139.pdf)

### Inferences
- **[EXTRAPOLATION]** Accurate wording for users:
  - **Can change:** maximal squeeze strength (EAS + puborectalis), how long a squeeze can be held, how fast and precisely it can be switched on (quick flicks), urge and flatus suppression, and conscious relaxation.
  - **Unlikely or unproven:** a permanent rise in resting tightness; a change in the anal opening's size or shape.
  - Sensations of "tone" between sessions are probably better awareness and control, not structural tightening.
- **[EXTRAPOLATION]** The app's exercise types map onto these mechanisms:
  - maximal slow holds → strength and possible hypertrophy (EAS/levator);
  - 1-s quick contractions → fast-twitch recruitment, rapid closure (cough, sprint, lift);
  - 50% long holds → endurance (postural, fatigue-resistant fibres);
  - elevator → graded control;
  - reverse Kegel → relaxation and avoiding hypertonicity.

  Each element has a mechanistic rationale, but none has been tested in healthy men as a package.
