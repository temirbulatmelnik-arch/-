# Functional and integrated PFMT, breathing and core coordination, relaxation/down-training, and the pelvic floor in athletes (end use: program for an athletic man, "Тонус")

Scope note for the report writer: most trials in this area are in women. Male-specific data are labelled **[MALE]**. Extrapolation from female populations is labelled **[FEMALE → extrapolated]**. Study design and size are given in brackets. Full texts on PubMed/PMC/MDPI could not be opened during this research (the fetch tool could not reach those hosts). Figures therefore come from abstracts, publisher pages and indexed summaries surfaced by search. Anything not confirmed that way is listed under Gaps.

## 1. The Knack (pre-contraction before cough, sneeze, lift): evidence and how to train it as a skill

### Takeaway
The Knack has small but consistent mechanistic and short-term evidence, all in women. In those studies, a voluntary pelvic floor contraction made just before and held through a cough cut leakage sharply within one week and reduced bladder-neck descent. In men, a related study shows that the striated urethral sphincter (SUS) normally switches on in proportion to rising abdominal pressure, but sometimes only after the pressure has started to rise. That gives a reasonable rationale for teaching a deliberate pre-contraction for heavy or unexpected loads. No trial tests the Knack in men.

### Cited Findings
- **Miller, Ashton-Miller & DeLancey 1998, J Am Geriatr Soc 46:870–874** [prospective, randomized, single-blind; n=27 women with mild–moderate SUI, mean age 68.0 ± 5.5 y] **[FEMALE]**. After 1 week of learning the Knack, urine loss on a medium cough fell by an average of **98.2%** compared with a similar cough 1 minute earlier without the Knack (P=.009). On a deep cough it fell by **73.3%** (P=.003). — [Wiley/JAGS](https://agsjournals.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/j.1532-5415.1998.tb02721.x); [Deep Blue repository](https://deepblue.lib.umich.edu/handle/2027.42/111199)
- **Miller et al. 2001, Obstet Gynecol** [experimental perineal-ultrasound study; n=22 women: 11 young continent nulliparas, 11 older incontinent parous women] **[FEMALE]**. A voluntary contraction during a cough reduced vesical-neck mobility from a median of **5.4 mm to 2.9 mm**. In the young women it fell from 4.6 to 0.0 mm, and in the older incontinent women from 6.2 to 3.5 mm. The authors concluded that a contraction "in preparation for, and throughout, a cough can augment proximal urethra support." — [PubMed 11165591](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/11165591)
- **Miller et al. 2008, Int Urogynecol J**, "Clarification and confirmation of the Knack maneuver: the effect of volitional pelvic floor muscle contraction to preempt expected stress incontinence". The paper was found but its results were not retrieved (see Gaps). — [Springer](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s00192-007-0525-3)
- **German perineal-ultrasound study, Geburtshilfe Frauenheilkd 2017** **[FEMALE]**. Without a voluntary contraction the bladder neck descended on average **2.3–4.4 mm**. With a pre-contraction it descended **0.5–2.1 mm**. The authors caution that a voluntary contraction "does not necessarily prevent bladder neck descent". — [Thieme](https://www.thieme-connect.de/products/ejournals/abstract/10.1055/s-0043-103460)
- In current RCT practice the Knack is taught as a standard part of programs. In Navarro-Brazález 2020, every arm (PFMT, hypopressive, combined) got the same education plus instruction in lifestyle interventions and the Knack. — [J Clin Med 2020 (PMC7230910)](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC7230910/)
- **[MALE] Stafford et al. 2012, Neurourol Urodyn 31:36–43** [experimental, n=5 healthy men aged 25–39; trans-urethral SUS EMG plus intra-abdominal pressure (IAP), standing]. Tasks were single and repetitive arm movement, stepping and load catching.
  - SUS activity rose in proportion to IAP (repetitive arm movement R² = **0.67–0.88**).
  - In stepping, SUS onset came before the IAP rise. With rapid arm movement and with unpredictable trunk loading, it generally came after the IAP rise.
  - The authors concluded that the SUS contributes to continence when IAP rises. Only young men were tested.
  — [PMC4118456](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4118456/); [Deep Blue PDF](https://deepblue.lib.umich.edu/bitstream/2027.42/90400/1/21178_ftp.pdf)
- **[MALE] Stafford et al. 2014, J Appl Physiol** [n=13 continent men, transperineal ultrasound]. Male pelvic floor contraction dynamics differ between voluntary and evoked coughs. Detailed timing was not retrieved. — [University of Melbourne record](https://findanexpert.unimelb.edu.au/scholarlywork/1189404-dynamics-of-male-pelvic-floor-muscle-contraction-observed-with-transperineal-ultrasound-imaging-differ-between-voluntary-and-evoked-coughs)
- Reflexive pelvic floor contractions have been recorded during coughing, running, trampolining and jumping, in a window from about **30 ms before to 150 ms after impact**. The authors of an RCT on "involuntary reflexive PFMT" call high-load impacts and high ground-reaction forces, arriving within milliseconds, the core triggers of SUI. **[FEMALE]** — [PMC8885550](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC8885550/)
- Patient-education descriptions of the Knack: squeeze right before and during coughing, sneezing, lifting and standing up. [patient education, not evidence] — [UroToday](https://www.urotoday.com/recent-abstracts/pelvic-health-reconstruction/nocturia/144977-doing-the-knack-to-stop-bladder-leaks.html)

### Inferences
- The app's tip "squeeze before cough, sneeze, lifting" matches the evidence. To make it a skill, the female data (learned within 1 week) and the male timing data suggest:
  - practice the contraction as a timed pre-load: contract, then cough or lift, then hold through the effort, then release;
  - first with voluntary coughs while seated, then standing, then during squat-to-stand, lifting a bag or box, and jumps;
  - keep breathing, and do not use a Valsalva in place of the squeeze.
- The male SUS lagged IAP during unpredictable loading (Stafford 2012). That suggests the voluntary pre-contraction matters most for sudden or heavy efforts (a heavy pull, sneeze, landing). For rhythmic, predictable tasks, automatic activity may already be adequate.
- In men the Knack has been studied mainly as urethral support. Its effect on anal or flatus control is plausible on mechanical grounds but has not been tested.

### Gaps
- The Miller 2008 "clarification and confirmation" results (sample, % reduction) were not retrieved.
- No study tests the Knack in healthy or athletic men, or tests it for anal or flatus continence.
- No trial was found on how many repetitions or how long it takes for the Knack to become automatic.

## 2. Breathing–pelvic floor coordination: physiology, exhale-coordinated contractions, breath-holding and Valsalva risk

### Takeaway
Dynamic MRI shows the diaphragm and pelvic floor moving in parallel: down on inhale, up on exhale and during cough. Resting pelvic floor EMG is mainly expiratory. This supports the app's cue (pelvic floor softens and descends on inhale) and makes contracting on exhale physiologically natural. However, no trial shows that exhale-timed PFMT gives better outcomes than ordinary breathing during contractions. The main consistent evidence is negative: breath-holding and straining are common errors, and Valsalva drives intra-abdominal pressure up to very high levels during heavy lifting.

### Cited Findings
- **Talasz et al. 2011, Int Urogynecol J 22(1):61–68**, "Phase-locked parallel movement of diaphragm and pelvic floor during breathing and coughing" [pilot dynamic MRI, 1.5 T, n=8 healthy nulliparous women, supine] **[FEMALE → extrapolated]**.
  - Diaphragm and pelvic floor moved in parallel: both descended on inspiration and rose on expiration.
  - Mean craniocaudal diaphragm excursion was about **15 mm (right) and 9 mm (left)** in quiet breathing, and about **32 and 28 mm** in forceful breathing and coughing.
  - Abdominal diameter decreased during expiration in all 8 women and during coughing in 5.
  — [Springer](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s00192-010-1240-z); [Springer Medizin abstract](https://www.springermedizin.de/phase-locked-parallel-movement-of-diaphragm-and-pelvic-floor-dur/8146228). A later review table lists it as a supine, healthy-volunteer study. — [PMC11207910 table](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11207910/table/sensors-24-04001-t006)
- **Hodges, Sapsford & Pengel 2007, Neurourol Urodyn 26(3):362–371** [experimental EMG, anal and vaginal surface electrodes].
  - Respiratory activity in pelvic floor EMG was "**primarily expiratory**".
  - Pelvic floor activity rose **before** deltoid activity as part of anticipatory postural control, regardless of arm-movement direction. Its size scaled with arm acceleration, i.e. with the reactive forces on the spine.
  - During combined breathing and arm movement, pelvic floor EMG followed the arm movement, with little respiratory modulation. Conclusion: the pelvic floor has both postural and respiratory functions.
  — [PubMed 17304528](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17304528)
- **J Appl Physiol 2019**, "Task-specific differences in respiration-related activation of deep and superficial pelvic floor muscles" [n=12 women] **[FEMALE]**. Both the deep and superficial pelvic floor layers were more active in expiration than inspiration, and more active during maximal than submaximal respiratory efforts. — [J Appl Physiol](https://journals.physiology.org/doi/full/10.1152/japplphysiol.00704.2018)
- **ICS 2023 abstract #648**, "Can you breathe yourself to a better pelvic floor? A systematic review" [conference abstract]. Pelvic floor activation during expiration alone was significantly **lower** than during a voluntary pelvic floor contraction. Breathing alone therefore does not substitute for active contraction. — [ICS 2023](https://www.ics.org/2023/abstract/648)
- **Breath-holding as an error:** in an RCT comparing verbal education, vaginal palpation and perineometer training, common errors were stopping breathing, enhanced inhaling, pelvic tilt and straining. Incorrect movements decreased after training in all three groups. **[FEMALE]** — [PMC11943932](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC11943932/)
- A narrative/conceptual review (Talasz et al., "Breathing, (S)Training and the Pelvic Floor—A Basic Concept") argues that holding the breath in an inspiratory pattern during exertion stresses the pelvic floor, because high IAP presses on relaxed, under-protected muscles. It also reports an Austrian sample in which **44.9%** of women could not contract the pelvic floor correctly and only **26.5%** could contract without raising IAP. [conceptual review; the primary data source of the percentages was not verified] — [ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/361045733_Breathing_STraining_and_the_Pelvic_Floor-A_Basic_Concept)
- **IAP during heavy resistance exercise with Valsalva** [systematic review]. IAP was highest in **squats (>200 mmHg)**, followed by deadlift, slide row and leg press (**161–176 mmHg**), and lowest in bench press (**79 ± 44 mmHg**). Intrathoracic pressure peaked in leg press, deadlift and box lift (**105–130 mmHg**). The authors advise untrained people not to use heavy squats, deadlifts, box lifts or cleans until they have adapted progressively. — [ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/337715315_Systematic_review_of_intra-abdominal_and_intrathoracic_pressures_initiated_by_the_Valsalva_manoeuvre_during_high-intensity_resistance_exercises); see also Hackett & Chow 2013, "The Valsalva maneuver: its effect on intra-abdominal pressure and safety issues during resistance exercise", J Strength Cond Res — [JSCR](https://journals.lww.com/nsca-jscr/fulltext/2013/08000/the_valsalva_maneuver__its_effect_on.39.aspx)
- For scale: a maximal voluntary pelvic floor contraction itself raised IAP by about **10 mmHg** (supine) in Neumann & Gill 2002 [n=4]. — [Springer](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s001920200027)

### Inferences
- The app's breathing block (inhale 4 s with diaphragm and pelvic floor descending, exhale 6 s) agrees with Talasz 2011 and with the expiratory bias of pelvic floor EMG. Caveat: the MRI data are from 8 supine women. No equivalent male MRI study was found.
- Timing contractions to the exhale is a reasonable teaching aid, especially for beginners and quick 1-s flicks. For 10-s maximal holds and 20–30-s submaximal holds, the user cannot stay on one exhale. The app should therefore say explicitly: "keep breathing normally during the hold; do not hold your breath or push down."
- Heavy lifting with Valsalva produces IAP (>150–200 mmHg) far above anything a voluntary pelvic floor contraction generates. For an athlete, the realistic aim is a pelvic floor that pre-tensions and keeps up with these loads (Knack plus fast, strong contractions), not one that avoids Valsalva entirely. This is inference: no male-specific evidence links Valsalva lifting to pelvic floor injury.

### Gaps
- No RCT compares exhale-timed with breath-independent PFMT on strength or continence outcomes.
- No direct study of how breath-holding changes the strength of a pelvic floor contraction was found.
- No male dynamic MRI of diaphragm–pelvic floor coupling was found. Male pelvic floor respiratory data are limited to anal EMG within mixed samples (Hodges 2007).

## 3. Co-activation with transversus abdominis (TrA); Bø's critique of indirect training; core, Pilates and yoga versus dedicated PFMT

### Takeaway
The deep abdominals co-activate with pelvic floor contractions, and in Neumann & Gill's small sample they could not be fully relaxed while contracting. Natural co-activation is normal. Using a TrA or "draw-in" contraction as the training stimulus is a different matter: it lifts the pelvic floor less than a direct pelvic floor contraction in women, works poorly as a cue in men, and has no RCT support as a replacement for PFMT. Pilates and yoga have not been shown to match PFMT. Adding a deliberate pelvic floor contraction to Pilates increases strength compared with Pilates alone.

### Cited Findings
- **Neumann & Gill 2002, Int Urogynecol J 13:125–132** [EMG plus IAP; n=4; supine and standing] **[FEMALE]**. TrA and internal oblique were recruited during **all** pelvic floor contractions. Subjects could not contract the pelvic floor effectively while keeping the deep abdominals relaxed. A maximal pelvic floor contraction raised IAP by about 10 mmHg (supine). — [Springer](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s001920200027)
- **Sapsford et al. 2001, Neurourol Urodyn 20:31–42**, "Co-activation of the abdominal and pelvic floor muscles during voluntary exercises", and **Sapsford & Hodges 2001, Arch Phys Med Rehabil 82:1081–1088**. Both were confirmed as the foundational co-activation papers, but their numerical results were not retrieved. Sapsford also published a 2010 systematic review, "Abdominal or pelvic floor muscle training", whose conclusions were not retrieved. — [Sapsford 2010, Wiley](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1002/nau.20861); [Springer chapter on abdominal–PFM interaction](https://link.springer.com/chapter/10.1007/978-3-030-94174-1_31)
- **Bø et al. 2003, transabdominal ultrasound** **[FEMALE]**. An instructed pelvic floor contraction produced **61.6% greater** pelvic floor displacement in the correct (cranial) direction than a TrA contraction. — [PubMed 12951667](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/12951667/)
- **Bø, Mørkved et al. 2009 systematic review (TrA for female UI)**:
  - no RCT compared TrA training with untreated or sham controls;
  - **two RCTs showed no additional effect** of adding TrA training to PFMT.
  — [PubMed 19191277](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19191277)
- **Bø & Herbert 2013, J Physiother 59(3):159–168** [systematic review; 7 RCTs: 3 abdominal training, 2 Paula method, 2 Pilates] **[FEMALE]**.
  - There was "no convincing evidence" for these regimens, which remain in the "testing phase".
  - Posture correction, breathing exercise, yoga, Tai Chi and general fitness training were placed in the "development phase", with no RCTs.
  - Conclusion: these alternative regimens should not yet be recommended for women with SUI.
  — [PubMed 23896331](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23896331/) (DOI 10.1016/S1836-9553(13)70180-2)
- **Bø 2004**, "Pelvic floor muscle training is effective in treatment of female stress urinary incontinence, but how does it work?", Int Urogynecol J 15(2):76–84. This is the theoretical basis of the critique (training should target the pelvic floor directly). The text itself was not retrieved; the citation was confirmed through reference lists. — [cited in Springer RCT reference list](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s00192-016-3037-1)
- **Assessor-masked RCT (Int Urogynecol J 2016/2017)** **[FEMALE]**. In sedentary nulliparous women, adding a voluntary pelvic floor contraction to a Pilates program improved pelvic floor strength more than Pilates alone. — [Springer](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s00192-016-3037-1)
- **Culligan et al. 2010, Int Urogynecol J 21(4):401–408**, RCT of PFMT versus Pilates for pelvic floor strength. Registration was found; numerical results were not retrieved. — [ClinicalTrials.gov NCT00549458](https://clinicaltrials.gov/study/NCT00549458)
- **Yoga versus Pilates versus PFMT in elderly women** [randomized pilot] **[FEMALE]**. Yoga beat Pilates on ICIQ-SF. Compared with PFMT, between-group differences on ICIQ-SF and the 1-h pad test were **not significant**. — [ScienceDirect](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S1744388121002012)
- **2026 RCT, Int Urogynecol J**, "Pelvic Floor Muscle Training Outperforms Pilates in Women with SUI". PFMT was superior to both Pilates groups on all outcomes. **[FEMALE]** — [Springer](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s00192-026-06900-4)
- Further recent RCTs exist but their results were not retrieved:
  - PFMT vs Pilates in postmenopausal women — [Springer 2023](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s00192-023-05712-0)
  - yoga- and Pilates-based respiratory training for UI — [PubMed 42171720](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42171720/)
  - PFMT plus high- vs low-intensity stabilization (PELSTAB) — [PMC7373635](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC7373635/)
- **[MALE] Ben Ami, Feldman & Dar 2022, IJERPH 19(19):12031** [experimental; n=35 healthy young male physiotherapy students; transabdominal ultrasound, crook lying; 6 verbal cues]. The **"draw in"** cue, which combines breathing, pelvic floor and TrA, produced upward bladder-base displacement in only **25%** of men and had the lowest endurance (p<0.001). The authors recommend **avoiding "draw in"** and the generic "squeeze your PFM" as teaching cues for men. — [PMC9566287](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC9566287/)

### Inferences
- For the app: let the abdominals co-activate naturally, since full abdominal relaxation is probably impossible (Neumann & Gill). The cue should still target the pelvic floor and anus directly, not the belly. Avoid instructions like "pull your navel in" as the main cue: in men it was the worst cue (Ben Ami 2022), and in women TrA training adds nothing to PFMT (Bø 2009).
- Pilates, yoga and core work are reasonable as general fitness or a context for practising the Knack. They should not replace the dedicated pelvic floor sets, and pelvic floor work should be layered into them explicitly. That mirrors the Pilates-plus-pelvic-floor RCT.
- All the Pilates, yoga and TrA trial evidence is in women, so applying it to men is extrapolation. The direction of effect, however, matches the one male cue study.

### Gaps
- Sapsford 2001 numbers and Sapsford 2010 SR conclusions were not retrieved.
- No RCT of Pilates, yoga or core programs versus PFMT in men was found.
- No study of TrA co-activation patterns specifically in athletic men was found.

## 4. Hypopressive exercise ("low-pressure fitness"): effective compared with PFMT?

### Takeaway
In women, hypopressive exercise (HE) is at best equal to PFMT and in the best-designed prolapse RCT it was inferior. It does not add to PFMT when combined. During HE the pelvic floor works at a submaximal level, which is likely insufficient for strength gains. No male data exist. Not recommended as a replacement for direct contractions in the app.

### Cited Findings
- **Resende et al. 2019, Neurourol Urodyn 38(1):171–179** [assessor-blinded RCT; women with symptomatic untreated stage II POP who could contract the pelvic floor; 12-week home programs plus bimonthly physiotherapist sessions; PEDro 6/10, no intention-to-treat; NCT01196598] **[FEMALE]**.
  - "PFMT was superior to HE for all outcomes."
  - PFMT gave better results for vaginal bulge, heaviness/dragging and stress incontinence.
  — [PubMed 30311680](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30311680/); [PEDro](https://search.pedro.org.au/search-results/record-detail/55085)
- **Bernardes/Resende et al. 2012, Sao Paulo Med J** [RCT; n=58 women, stage II POP; PFMT vs HE vs control]. Levator-ani cross-sectional area improved within the PFMT and HE groups, but **not significantly compared with control**. The authors called the two methods similar. [small, imaging outcome only] — [ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/221841855_Efficacy_of_pelvic_floor_muscle_training_and_hypopressive_exercises_for_treating_pelvic_organ_prolapse_in_women_randomized_controlled_trial)
- **Navarro-Brazález et al. 2020, J Clin Med 9:1149** [single-centre assessor-blinded RCT; n=94 women with pelvic floor dysfunction; PFMT n=32, HE n=31, PFMT+HE n=31; all arms received education and the Knack; follow-up to 12 months; PEDro 7/10].
  - Outcomes: PFDI-20, PFIQ-7, manometry/dynamometry strength and basal tone.
  - **No significant differences between groups** after the intervention.
  - The combination was not superior, despite the trial registration's hypothesis that it would be best.
  — [PMC7230910](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC7230910/); [PEDro record](https://search.pedro.org.au/search-results/record-detail/60735); [NCT02259712](https://clinicaltrials.gov/study/NCT02259712)
- **Pelvic floor activity during HE** [cross-sectional; n=66 women with pelvic floor dysfunction]. Pelvic floor sEMG during HE averaged about **61% of maximal voluntary contraction (MVC) standing and about 74–87% supine**. The authors judged this probably insufficient for strength gains, with a possible endurance effect. — [Univ. Alcalá repository](https://ebuah.uah.es/xmlui/handle/10017/64470)
- **Stüpp et al. 2011, Neurourol Urodyn 30:1518–1521** [sEMG; 34 nulliparous female physiotherapists]. HE activated TrA less than a pelvic floor contraction did, and adding a pelvic floor contraction to HE increased TrA activation. A companion ICS abstract (n=33 healthy women) concluded that HE "did not improve pelvic floor contraction", though it might reflexively activate the pelvic floor early on. A clinic blog claims Stüpp showed strengthening; the abstracts do not support that. — [Wiley](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1002/nau.21151); [ICS 2010 abstract 588](https://www.ics.org/2010/abstract/588)
- **Juez et al. 2019, Neurourol Urodyn** [prospective cohort, postpartum]. HE versus PFMT: no significant difference in maximal strength change. [non-randomized] — [Wiley](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1002/nau.24094)
- **Soriano et al. 2020, Physiotherapy** [randomized crossover]. A 2-month HE program showed short-term benefits for pelvic floor tone and UI versus the control period. — [ScienceDirect](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0031940620300183)
- **Molina-Torres et al. 2023, Neurourol Urodyn** [RCT; 8-week HE]. Pelvic floor symptoms decreased after HE compared with control. Not compared against PFMT. — [Wiley](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1002/nau.25110)
- A systematic review of RCTs on HE in adult women (2023, J Bodyw Mov Ther) and a 2023 Bø et al. review on HE for POP (Int Urogynecol J) were found; their conclusions were not retrieved. — [ScienceDirect SR](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S1360859223001791)

### Inferences
- HE may beat doing nothing, but it does not beat PFMT and adds nothing on top of it. It sends the pelvic floor submaximal activation, which conflicts with the app's goal of maximal strength and tone.
- HE also involves apnea (holding the breath after exhaling). That adds no clear benefit for an athlete who already does direct PFMT. Recommendation: do not add HE to "Тонус". At most, mention it as optional mobility or breath-work, not as pelvic floor training.

### Gaps
- No HE study in men was found.
- The Martín-Rodríguez & Bø 2019 BJSM editorial ("Is abdominal hypopressive technique effective in the prevention and treatment of pelvic floor dysfunction? Marketing or evidence from high-quality clinical trials?") could not be retrieved, so it is not cited for content.
- The conclusions of the 2023 systematic reviews were not retrieved.

## 5. Specificity of position and function: do standing, functional and impact-loaded contractions transfer better?

### Takeaway
Direct trials of "functional PFMT" (training during squats, jumps, lifts) against classical PFMT have been registered but their results are not available. The supporting evidence is mechanistic.
- The pelvic floor fires anticipatorily with limb movement.
- In men, the urethral sphincter scales with abdominal pressure in standing dynamic tasks.
- Leakage in sport is driven by impacts lasting milliseconds.
- Stress leakage is reported to be more common standing than in other postures.
Together these justify progressing to standing and task-specific training plus fast and reflexive contractions. Proof of superior transfer is lacking.

### Cited Findings
- **Hodges et al. 2007** [EMG]. Pelvic floor activity is part of the pre-programmed anticipatory postural response. It starts before the deltoid and scales with arm acceleration. — [PubMed 17304528](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17304528)
- **[MALE] Stafford et al. 2012** [n=5 healthy men, standing; arm movements, stepping, load catching]. SUS activity was linearly related to IAP (R² 0.67–0.88). Onset preceded IAP in stepping and lagged it in rapid arm movement and unpredictable loading. — [PMC4118456](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4118456/)
- **[MALE] Cowley, Stafford & Hodges 2020, Neurourol Urodyn 39(3)**, "Influence of body position on dynamics of the pelvic floor measured with transperineal ultrasound imaging in men" (doi 10.1002/nau.24301). The paper was confirmed but its results were not retrieved. A related ICS 2021 abstract (32 men, seated vs standing, post-prostatectomy context) notes that stress incontinence is reported to be more common standing, yet male pelvic floor function is rarely assessed standing. — [Wiley](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1002/nau.24301); [ICS 2021 abstract 296](https://www.ics.org/2021/abstract/296)
- **Reflexive and impact model** [RCT of involuntary reflexive PFMT added to standard training] **[FEMALE]**.
  - Fast involuntary reflexive contractions were seen in coughing, running, trampolining and jumping, from 30 ms before to 150 ms after impact.
  - The authors argue future training should target hypertrophy, intramuscular coordination and **power**, not only voluntary endurance.
  — [PMC8885550](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC8885550/)
- **Registered RCT: "Functional Pelvic Floor Muscle Training" vs classical PFMT** [n=41; classical arm uses maximal and submaximal contractions with sets added every 2 weeks]. No results posted in the retrieved record. — [NCT05293886](https://clinicaltrials.gov/study/NCT05293886)
- The general conclusion of Bø & Herbert 2013 also applies: posture- and function-based alternatives without direct PFMT lack RCT support. — [PubMed 23896331](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23896331/)

### Inferences
Suggested progression for an athletic man. This is inference, consistent with motor-learning principles and the mechanistic data above, not tested as a package.
1. Seated or supine isolation, as the app's levels 1–4 already do.
2. Standing isolation, as levels 5–7 already do.
3. Standing plus movement, using the Knack during:
   - sit-to-stand, squat-to-stand and hip hinge;
   - lifting a light bag;
   - voluntary coughs;
   - walking.
4. Load and impact: brief maximal contractions before and during:
   - light jumps or skipping;
   - a moderate lift;
   - an exhale-on-effort set.
5. Optional: "flicks" emphasising speed (fast-twitch) to match impact timing.

Isolated sets should be kept throughout. Every positive trial is built on them, and functional add-ons have no proof of superiority.

### Gaps
- No completed RCT comparing functional or impact-loaded PFMT with classical PFMT (in either sex) was retrieved.
- Cowley 2020 male position results were not retrieved.
- No study tests transfer of male pelvic floor training to sport-specific continence.

## 6. Athletes and strength-trained people: prevalence, IAP, whether training strengthens or overloads the pelvic floor, recommendations

### Takeaway
Female athletes, especially in high-impact and heavy lifting sports, have high rates of urinary incontinence. Rates in men are much lower: about 6% in CrossFit, 9.3% in Norwegian competitive lifters, and 3.8–18.8% for LUTS across male-athlete studies. Two signals matter for an anal-sphincter-focused app:
- Anal incontinence was reported by **61.8% of male powerlifters and Olympic weightlifters** in one survey (definitions likely include flatus).
- Lifting with Valsalva generates intra-abdominal pressure above 150–200 mmHg.
Exercise does not reliably train the pelvic floor in a targeted way. Bø & Nygaard conclude the evidence partly supports both the "training" and the "overload" hypotheses, and no formal male-athlete pelvic floor consensus statement exists.

### Cited Findings
- **Bø & Nygaard 2020, Sports Med 50(3):471–484** [narrative review] **[FEMALE]**. Two opposing hypotheses: (1) exercise strengthens the pelvic floor and protects against UI, AI and POP; (2) exercise overloads and stretches the pelvic floor and raises those risks. Findings support aspects of both:
  - exercising women have similar or stronger pelvic floor strength;
  - mild-to-moderate activity such as walking is linked to lower UI risk;
  - **female athletes show higher UI rates**;
  - evidence that strenuous exercise causes or worsens POP is inconsistent.
  — [Springer](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s40279-019-01243-1); [PMC7018791](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC7018791/); [NIH Brage repository](https://nih.brage.unit.no/nih-xmlui/handle/11250/2653711)
- **[MALE] Skaug, Engh, Frawley & Bø 2022, J Strength Cond Res 36(10):2800–2807** [cross-sectional survey; ICIQ questionnaires; all Norwegian national-championship powerlifters and Olympic weightlifters 2018/19; 180 women, 204 men].
  - **Men:** UI **9.3%**, anal incontinence **61.8%**.
  - **Women:** UI 50.0% (SUI 41.7%), AI 80.0%, POP symptoms 23.3%.
  - In women, BMI was associated with SUI and international-level competition with AI.
  - Athletes had limited knowledge of the pelvic floor.
  — [PubMed 33278274](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33278274/); [LIDA record](https://lida.sport-iat.de/ta/Record/4077519)
- **[MALE] CrossFit survey** [n=571 practitioners]. UI in about **45% of women and 6% of men**. "Male athletes can also experience urine leakage while performing CrossFit®." — [PubMed 35905542](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35905542/)
- **[MALE] Myers, Doma, Cooke & Nahon 2025/2026, J Sci Med Sport 29:351–359**, "Prevalence of pelvic floor dysfunction in male athletes and its dose-dependency in high-intensity exercise" [scoping review; 4493 screened, 10 included].
  - Six studies gave LUTS (including UI) prevalence of **3.8–18.8%**.
  - Athletic men may have higher LUTS rates than the general male population.
  - Higher MET-minute volumes were linked to more LUTS but **lower** erectile dysfunction and CP/CPPS risk.
  - Exercise variables were reported inconsistently.
  — [PubMed 41198451](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41198451/); [UroToday](https://www.urotoday.com/recent-abstracts/pelvic-health-reconstruction/stress-incontinence/164501-prevalence-of-pelvic-floor-dysfunction-in-male-athletes-and-its-dose-dependency-in-high-intensity-exercise-a-scoping-review.html) (doi 10.1016/j.jsams.2025.10.008)
- **Giagio et al. 2021, Neurourol Urodyn** [scoping review].
  - 100 studies included, but **only 12 explored pelvic floor dysfunction in men**.
  - Reported PFD rates were higher in females (n=103, 53.7%) than males (n=39, 29.3%). The exact meaning of these n values was not clear from the abstract.
  - Bodybuilding/CrossFit and running appeared to be the riskiest sports.
  — [Wiley](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/abs/10.1002/nau.24564); [ICS 2020 abstract 360](https://www.ics.org/2020/abstract/360)
- **Power- and weightlifters PFMT pilot** [pilot]. Notes that recent prevalence studies among power- and weightlifters report UI of **32–50%**, mostly women. Results of the pilot itself were not retrieved. — [PMC11245411](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC11245411/)
- **Skaug et al. 2024, Med Sci Sports Exerc 56(1):37–43**, acute effect of heavy weightlifting on the pelvic floor [randomized crossover; about 46–47 nulliparous strength-trained women; vaginal resting pressure, MVC and sEMG before and after lifting vs 60-min seated rest]. Outcome direction was not retrieved. **[FEMALE]** — [ICS 2023 abstract 282](https://www.ics.org/2023/abstract/282)
- **IAP during lifting with Valsalva** [systematic review]: squats >200 mmHg; deadlift, slide row and leg press 161–176 mmHg; bench press 79 ± 44 mmHg. — [ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/337715315_Systematic_review_of_intra-abdominal_and_intrathoracic_pressures_initiated_by_the_Valsalva_manoeuvre_during_high-intensity_resistance_exercises)
- **Youth athletes, Lima 2024 World Athletics U20 Championships** [cross-sectional, n=325]. **44%** reported at least one pelvic floor symptom and **88%** had never been screened. The male/female split was not retrieved. — [PMC12306240](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC12306240/); [World Athletics](https://worldathletics.org/news/news/international-research-youth-athletes-pelvic-floor-dysfunction)
- **Consensus documents:** the only formal sports consensus found is the **PFD-SENTINEL Delphi screening tool for female athletes** (BJSM 2023).
  - 5 PFD symptoms plus 28 risk/clinical/sport items.
  - Direct referral is recommended if at least 1 symptom or at least 14 items are present.
  - The >67% agreement threshold was set in the protocol.
  No equivalent for men was found. — [medRxiv protocol](https://www.medrxiv.org/content/10.1101/2022.01.12.22269000.full.pdf). Related: BJSM 2023 infographic "Ten naked truths about the pelvic floor in athletes" (content not retrieved) — [ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/373674094_Infographic_Ten_naked_truths_about_the_pelvic_floor_in_athletes); ACSM Current Sports Medicine Reports, "Sports Medicine and the Pelvic Floor" (content not retrieved) — [Ovid](https://www.ovid.com/jnls/acsm-csmr/fulltext/10.1249/jsr.0000000000001045~sports-medicine-and-the-pelvic-floor)
- **Knowledge gap in athletes:** a scoping review on athletes' knowledge of pelvic floor dysfunction and their engagement with PFMT exists (details not retrieved). — [PMC11764918](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11764918/)

### Inferences
- For a 190 cm / 90 kg athletic man, the base rate of urinary leakage is low (single digits). Anal and flatus control problems, however, appeared in the majority of male competitive lifters in the one survey that measured them. That directly supports the app's anal-sphincter emphasis and a "Knack before heavy lifts" module.
- The male AI figure (61.8%) almost certainly uses a broad ICIQ-B-type definition, probably including flatus and possibly minor soiling. The report should present it as "any anal incontinence symptom", not severe fecal incontinence. The definition was not verified.
- General strength training should not be assumed to train the pelvic floor in a targeted way (Bø & Nygaard; Bø & Herbert). Dedicated PFMT plus a pre-contraction strategy is the evidence-consistent approach.
- Warn users that heavy Valsalva lifting places large loads on the pelvic floor. Suggest pre-contracting before the brace on max attempts, and stopping or seeking assessment if leakage, perineal heaviness or anal pain appear with lifting.

### Gaps
- No IOC, ACSM or BJSM consensus statement on the **male** athlete pelvic floor was found. The 2016 IOC expert group meeting papers focus on pregnancy and were not retrieved.
- No study shows whether heavy strength training acutely or chronically changes **male** pelvic floor strength or tone. The Skaug 2024 acute data are female and their direction was not retrieved.
- No intervention trial of PFMT in male athletes was found.
- The anal incontinence definition in Skaug 2022 was not verified.

## 7. Relaxation, down-training and the "reverse Kegel": overactivity, conditions, risk of hypertonicity from excessive Kegels, placement and ratio of relaxation, warning signs

### Takeaway
Impaired pelvic floor relaxation is documented in men with chronic prostatitis/chronic pelvic pain syndrome (CP/CPPS). Relaxation- and coordination-based therapies are effective in specific conditions:
- myofascial physiotherapy and paradoxical relaxation for pelvic pain;
- biofeedback that teaches pelvic floor relaxation during straining for dyssynergic defecation.
Chronic anal fissure is linked to *internal* (involuntary) anal sphincter hypertonia. The claim that "too many Kegels cause hypertonicity" rests on clinical opinion, not trials. No RCT of the "reverse Kegel" or "pelvic floor drop" as a standalone exercise in healthy men was found. Including a relaxation phase is still justified: complete release after each contraction is the specific ability lost in CP/CPPS.

### Cited Findings
- **[MALE] Yani et al. 2022, Phys Ther 102(7), doi 10.1093/ptj/pzac059** [case-control; 42 men with CP/CPPS vs 48 men without pelvic pain; pelvic floor EMG].
  - Controls returned pelvic floor activity to baseline after voluntary contractions. Men with CP/CPPS did not: their resting activity was higher when resting **between contractions** than when simply resting.
  - The effect was largest in men with **ejaculatory pain**, who made up 70% of the CP/CPPS group.
  - The authors suggest physiotherapy aimed at improving pelvic floor relaxation as a mechanism for pain reduction.
  — [UroToday abstract](https://www.urotoday.com/recent-abstracts/men-s-health/prostatitis/137529-impaired-ability-to-relax-pelvic-floor-muscles-in-men-with-chronic-prostatitis-chronic-pelvic-pain-syndrome.html)
- **[MALE + FEMALE] FitzGerald et al., multicentre pilot RCT** [n≈48 with CP/CPPS or interstitial cystitis; 23 men (49%), 24 women; up to 10 weekly 1-h sessions].
  - Global response: **57% with myofascial physical therapy vs 21% with global therapeutic massage** (p=0.03).
  - One summary reports that the between-arm difference was significant mainly in the interstitial cystitis subgroup. Male-only results were not retrieved.
  — [PubMed 23234638](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23234638/); [PMC2872169](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2872169)
- **[MALE] Anderson, Wise et al. 2005, J Urol**, myofascial trigger-point release plus **paradoxical relaxation training** [uncontrolled case series; n=138 treatment-refractory men with CP/CPPS; NIH-CPSI and Pelvic Pain Symptom Survey]. Outcome figures were not retrieved. A 2006 follow-up from the same program (n=146 men) reported **70% marked or moderate global improvement** with significant NIH-CPSI drops. [no control group] — [PubMed 15947608](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/15947608/); [QxMD 2006 follow-up](https://www.qxmd.com/r/16952676)
- **Dyssynergic defecation (relaxation and coordination training via biofeedback)** [mixed-sex RCTs and cohorts]:
  - **Rao et al. 2007** [sham-controlled RCT]: >20% improvement in global satisfaction and stool frequency in **88% with biofeedback vs 48% with sham**, but not significantly better than standard care (70%). — [ScienceDirect](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S1542356506013218)
  - Biofeedback eliminated the dyssynergic pattern in **91%** (Chiarioni) and **92%, 12/13** (Rao). Benefit was seen in dyssynergia, not in isolated slow transit. — [Gastroenterology editorial](https://www.gastrojournal.org/article/S0016-5085(11)00374-X/fulltext)
  - **Rao et al. 2010** long-term RCT: biofeedback was more likely than standard therapy to keep bowel function normal at 1 year (13/13 completed biofeedback; 7/13 completed standard therapy). — [ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/41548743_Long-Term_Efficacy_of_Biofeedback_Therapy_for_Dyssynergic_Defecation_Randomized_Controlled_Trial)
  - Cohort of 127: treatment success in 61%, dyssynergia corrected in 78%, bowel satisfaction improved in 64%. — [Clin Gastroenterol Hepatol](https://www.cghjournal.org/article/S1542-3565(17)31294-6/fulltext)
- **Chronic anal fissure: internal anal sphincter (IAS) hypertonia** [manometry and endosonography cohorts; reviews]:
  - In 63 patients, IAS hypertonia (≥80 mmHg) was present in **66%**, mean resting pressure **91 ± 28 mmHg**, and the IAS was abnormally thick in **92%**. — [Springer Medicine](https://www.springermedicine.com/endosonographic-and-manometric-evaluation-of-internal-anal-sphin/20919084)
  - Lateral internal sphincterotomy reduced resting pressure from **138 ± 28 to 86 ± 15 mmHg** at 1 month.
  - The posterior anal commissure is poorly perfused in 85% (post-mortem angiography); hypertonia may worsen this ischaemia.
  - Not all studies find elevated pressures. — [PMC3140330](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3140330); [review summary](https://nutrition-evidence.com/article/47092/concepts-in-pathogenesis-and-treatment-of-chronic-anal-fissure-a-review-of-the-literature)
- **[MALE] Post-prostatectomy retrospective** [n=136; 13 had purely overactive pelvic floor muscles]. A subset may need **down-training rather than Kegel up-training** for best continence recovery. — [Renal & Urology News](https://www.renalandurologynews.com/features/kegel-exercises-after-prostate-surgery-called-into-question/)
- **"Excessive Kegels cause hypertonicity": clinical opinion only.**
  - Harvard Health quotes a urologist saying self-directed Kegels sometimes made things worse. Pain with intercourse is more often linked to an overly tight pelvic floor, where Kegels are not recommended. [expert opinion] — [Harvard Health](https://www.health.harvard.edu/healthy-aging-and-longevity/pelvic-floor-exercises-help-for-incontinence-sexual-health-and-more)
  - The commonly cited clinical review is Faubion et al. 2012, "Recognition and management of nonrelaxing pelvic floor dysfunction" (Mayo Clin Proc). It was found only as a citation in lay media and was not verified. — [Healthline](https://www.healthline.com/health/parenting/yes-you-really-can-do-too-many-kegels-heres-what-happens)
  - Clinic and blog sources list excessive Kegels as one possible contributor alongside stress, habitual holding and trauma, and recommend diaphragmatic breathing plus "reverse Kegels" as first-line for hypertonicity. [low-quality, commercial] — [pelvicare.uk](https://pelvicare.uk/blog/hypertonic-pelvic-floor-symptoms-causes-and-treatment); [omniclinic.ca](https://omniclinic.ca/blog/why-tight-pelvic-floor-muscles-cause-pain-and-kegels-wont-help)
- **ICS 2021 terminology (Frawley et al., Neurourol Urodyn 40(5):1217–1260, doi 10.1002/nau.24658)**. Digital palpation findings should be limited to "increased", "normal" or "decreased" tone. Tenderness and tone should be rated separately rather than lumped as "trigger points". Exact definitions of "overactive" and "non-relaxing" were not retrieved. — [University of Melbourne record](https://findanexpert.unimelb.edu.au/scholarlywork/1513286-an-international-continence-society-%28ics%29-report-on-the-terminology-for-pelvic-floor-muscle-assessment); [IUGA newsletter](https://www.iuga.org/spotlight-v16-6/physio-corner-tenderness-tone-trigger-point-overactivity-what-s-in-a-word)
- **Reverse Kegel as a named exercise:** no RCT found. Web content (e.g., claims for premature ejaculation, 4–8-week timelines) is unreferenced and commercial. — [search context: Doctronic](https://www.doctronic.ai/blog/reverse-kegel-for-men/) (cited only to flag low quality)
- **[MALE] Registered RCT of pelvic floor training in male chronic pelvic pain** (single-blind, 1:1, biofeedback/urodynamics). Results unknown. — [NCT06115083](https://clinicaltrials.gov/study/NCT06115083)

### Inferences
- **Keep the relaxation phase, and reframe it.** The best male evidence (Yani 2022) shows the dysfunction is failure to return to baseline *between* contractions. So relaxation should not only sit at the end of a session: every hold should be followed by a full, conscious release before the next one. Suggested cue: "let go completely, feel the anus and perineum soften; no pushing". This is inference; no trial has tested where in a session relaxation should go.
- **"Reverse Kegel" must not become straining.** Bump 1991 (Section 8) shows bearing down is a common error. The app's current wording ("on inhale let the perineum soften and descend, no straining") is appropriate. A gentle, passive descent with the breath is safer than an active "push out", which resembles the Valsalva error.
- **Rest-to-work ratio:** no trial evidence was found for an optimal ratio. As inference, give rest at least equal to hold time (e.g., 10 s hold followed by ≥10 s release), and longer after maximal holds, so complete relaxation can be checked each time.
- **Anal-sphincter focus caveat:** voluntary training targets the *external* anal sphincter and puborectalis (striated). Fissure-related hypertonia is in the *internal* (smooth, involuntary) sphincter. Kegels are not a treatment for fissure, and users with anal pain, bleeding or a known fissure should be told to see a doctor and skip strong anal squeezes until assessed. This is inference from the fissure physiology above; no trial of PFMT in fissure patients was found.
- **Warning signs** that should trigger "stop the high-intensity work, do relaxation only, see a pelvic health physiotherapist or urologist". These are clinical signs consistent with the CP/CPPS, dyssynergia and fissure literature above, not validated as an app screen:
  - perineal, testicular, penile or rectal pain or aching, especially after sessions;
  - pain with or after ejaculation;
  - hesitant or slow urine stream, or a feeling of incomplete emptying;
  - new straining or incomplete evacuation at stool, or constipation;
  - anal pain or bleeding;
  - a sense of constant "clenching" that cannot be released.
- **Volume:** the app's 3 sessions/day is a high volume. There is no evidence that it causes overactivity, but the release-to-baseline check is the safeguard. If symptoms above appear, reduce to relaxation-dominant sessions.

### Gaps
- No RCT tests reverse Kegels or "pelvic floor drops" as a standalone intervention in any population.
- No RCT or cohort shows that PFMT *causes* pelvic floor overactivity or pain. The claim remains clinical opinion.
- No evidence-based optimal ratio of contraction to relaxation, or placement of relaxation within a session, was found.
- Male-only FitzGerald results and the numerical outcomes of Anderson/Wise 2005 were not retrieved.
- The exact ICS 2021 definition of "overactive pelvic floor" was not retrieved.

## 8. Motor control and learning: teaching a correct contraction; cues that work for men

### Takeaway
Brief verbal instruction alone fails for many people. In Bump 1991, only 49% of women produced an ideal contraction and 25% produced one that could worsen incontinence by bearing down. In men, cues change which part of the pelvic floor works:
- **"Shorten the penis"** best activates the striated urethral sphincter (SUS) and lifts the mid-urethra.
- **"Tighten around the anus"** best activates the anal sphincter.
- "Squeeze your anus", "shorten the penis" and "elevate the scrotum" each get over 91% of young men to lift the bladder base correctly.
- "Draw in" and a generic "squeeze your pelvic floor" work poorly.
For an anal-sphincter-focused app, "squeeze/tighten around the anus" (as if holding in wind) is the primary cue, with "shorten the penis / lift the testicles" as secondary cues for front-of-pelvis control.

### Cited Findings
- **Bump et al. 1991, Am J Obstet Gynecol 165:322–329** [n=47 women; urethral pressure profilometry after brief standardized verbal instruction] **[FEMALE]**.
  - **49% (23/47)** produced an ideal effort that raised urethral closure force without meaningful Valsalva.
  - **25% (12/47)** used a technique that could potentially **worsen** incontinence (straining).
  - Age, parity, weight, estrogen status and previous surgery did not predict success.
  - Conclusion: simple verbal or written instruction is not adequate preparation for a Kegel program.
  — [University of Minnesota abstract](https://experts.umn.edu/en/publications/assessment-of-kegel-pelvic-muscle-exercise-performance-after-brie/). Commentary notes the small sample and arbitrary definition of "effective". — [Herman & Wallace](https://hermanwallace.com/blog/pelvic-muscle-contraction-after-verbal-instruction)
- **[MALE] Stafford, Ashton-Miller, Constantinou, Coughlin, Lutton & Hodges 2016, Neurourol Urodyn 35(4):457–463** [experimental; n=15 healthy men; transperineal ultrasound plus surface EMG of abdominals and anal sphincter; in 3 men also fine-wire EMG of puborectalis and bulbocavernosus and trans-urethral SUS EMG; submaximal contractions with different cues, including "tighten around the anus", "elevate the bladder", "shorten the penis"].
  - **"Shorten the penis" produced the greatest dorsal displacement of the mid-urethra and the most SUS activity.**
  - **"Tighten around the anus" produced the greatest anal sphincter activity.**
  - Conclusion: instructions change the pattern of activation, and training efficacy may depend on the instruction used.
  - Whether "stop the flow of urine" was tested could not be confirmed.
  — [Wiley](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1002/nau.22745); [Stanford profile listing](https://profiles.stanford.edu/christos-constantinou)
- **[MALE] Ben Ami, Feldman & Dar 2022, IJERPH 19(19):12031** [n=35 healthy young men; transabdominal ultrasound; crook lying; 6 cues].
  - **"Squeeze your anus", "shorten the penis" and "elevate the scrotum" each produced upward bladder-base displacement in >91% of men.**
  - "Draw in" managed only 25%, with the lowest endurance (p<0.001).
  - The authors advise against "draw in" and generic "squeeze your PFM".
  — [PMC9566287](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC9566287/)
- **Ben Ami & Dar 2018 (women, comparator)**. "Squeeze the anus" gave **90%** correct contraction vs **65%** for "stop the flow of urine". **[FEMALE]** — [World Physiotherapy congress proceeding](https://world.physio/congress-proceeding/what-most-effective-verbal-cue-good-contraction-pelvic-floor-muscles)
- **[MALE] Stafford et al. 2017, BJU Int**: shear-wave elastography can estimate male SUS stiffness during voluntary contractions. Details were not retrieved. This is a potential future objective measure of "tone". — [BJU Int](https://bjui-journals.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/bju.13688)
- **[MALE] Stafford et al. 2022, Neurourol Urodyn**: pre- and post-prostatectomy pelvic floor function measures related to early incontinence. Details were not retrieved. — [PMC9826381](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9826381/)
- **Physiotherapy management of incontinence in men** (J Physiother 2021 review): details not retrieved. — [ScienceDirect](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S183695532100014X)
- **Teaching-method RCT (women)**: errors (breath-holding, enhanced inhaling, pelvic tilt, straining) decreased after training in all three teaching arms (verbal, palpation, perineometer). — [PMC11943932](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC11943932/)

### Inferences
- **Primary cue for "Тонус"**: "squeeze and lift around the anus, as if holding in wind". It maximizes anal sphincter activity (Stafford 2016) and reliably lifts the pelvic floor in men (Ben Ami 2022).
- **Secondary cue**, to train the anterior pelvic floor and urethral sphincter, which also matters for post-void dribble and urinary control: "shorten the penis / draw the testicles up". Alternating cue emphasis across sets could train both regions. This is inference; no trial compares single-cue and alternating-cue programs.
- **Self-check for correct technique**, because about 1 in 4 people bear down instead (Bump 1991, female data):
  - the base of the penis retracts slightly and the testicles rise; the anus pulls in, not out;
  - the buttocks, inner thighs and breath stay relaxed;
  - no bulging of the perineum.
- An optional mirror or palpation check of the perineum (it should lift, not bulge) is a low-tech biofeedback substitute.
- Avoid the "stop the flow of urine" cue as a practice method, i.e. actually stopping urination. In women it performed worse than the anal cue (Ben Ami 2018). Whether it works in men was not confirmed from Stafford 2016.

### Gaps
- The full Stafford 2016 results (effect sizes; whether "stop the flow of urine" or "hold in wind" were tested) were not retrieved.
- No trial in men compares outcomes (strength, continence) from programs taught with different cues. The evidence is limited to acute activation patterns.
- No male study quantifies the proportion who strain or bear down when given verbal instruction, i.e. a male equivalent of Bump 1991.
