# PFMT dosage, training parameters and strength-training science applied to the pelvic floor and anal sphincter

Evidence-level tags used throughout: **[MA]** meta-analysis, **[SR]** systematic/scoping review, **[Cochrane]** Cochrane review, **[RCT]**, **[Cohort/FU]** follow-up of a trial cohort, **[Pilot]** small uncontrolled study, **[Phys]** physiology/lab study, **[Guideline]**, **[Expert]** expert opinion/narrative.
**Population flag:** unless marked **(MEN)**, data come from women, mostly with stress urinary incontinence (SUI). Applying them to a healthy athletic man training the anal sphincter is an extrapolation.
**Method note:** The egress proxy blocked direct full-text fetches (PMC, NCBI, ICS, Brage). All numbers below come from abstracts, tables and snippets returned by web search. Where a commonly quoted number could not be checked, it is listed under Gaps rather than reported as fact.

## Q1. What dosage parameters did the most successful PFMT trials and reviews use, and is there an optimal number of contractions, sessions, hold duration or program length?

### Takeaway
No trial has found one optimal dose. The protocols that work best converge on 8–12 near-maximal contractions per set, about 3 sets a day (24–36+ contractions a day), with 6–10 s holds, run for at least 12 weeks and preferably 5–6 months, with regular supervision. Meta-analyses show a threshold rather than a dose-response curve: at least 24 contractions a day for at least 6 weeks. Above that, more contractions did not predict a bigger effect.

### Cited Findings

**Guidelines / consensus "minimum dose"**
- [Guideline] NICE (NG123, carried over from CG171): offer a trial of *supervised* PFMT of **at least 3 months** as first-line treatment for stress or mixed UI. Training should include **at least 8 contractions performed 3 times per day**, and continue longer if it helps — [NIHR Evidence summary](https://evidence.nihr.ac.uk/alert/pelvic-floor-muscle-training-can-improve-symptoms-of-urinary-incontinence/); [NICE QS77 statement 4](https://www.nice.org.uk/guidance/qs77/chapter/Quality-statement-4-Supervised-pelvic-floor-muscle-training)
- [Guideline] A pharmacy CPD summary of NICE gives the same regimen as **"at least eight contractions three times a day for a minimum of 12 weeks"**, with abdominal, leg and buttock muscles kept relaxed — [Pharmacy Magazine CPD](https://www.pharmacymagazine.co.uk/cpd-modules/update-on-incontinence/behavioural-changes-and-physical-therapies-811591)
- [Guideline] For prolapse, NICE NG123 is summarised as **16 weeks** of supervised PFMT for symptomatic stage 1–2 POP — [IUGA symposium slides, K. Lough](https://www.iuga.org/images/content/meeting/International_Symposia/1145-conservative-management-of-sui-and-pop-kate-lough.pdf)
- [Guideline] NICE notes that many women given only a leaflet had been doing the exercises *incorrectly for years with no improvement*. This is the rationale for supervision — [NICE QS77](https://www.nice.org.uk/guidance/QS77/chapter/quality-statement-4-supervised-pelvic-floor-muscle-training)

**Bø's protocols (Norway; the most-cited "strength-training" PFMT trials)**
- [Expert/SR] Bø's summary of PFM strength-training principles: specific training of the target muscle; **"three sets of between eight and 12 exercises close to maximum contraction per day, three or four times per week"**; continued for **6 months**. Effective regimens used **supervised training once a week plus home training on all other days** — [Bø, POGP/ACPWH journal 2011, "Can PFMT prevent and treat POP?"](https://thepogp.co.uk/journal/21/acpwh_journal_108_spring_2011/368)
- [Expert] Bø's 1995 exercise-physiology review: exercise-science guidance implies **8–12 contractions in three series, 3–4 times a week, for 15–20 weeks or more**. Significant strength gains have been measured after programs of **3 to 6 months** — [Bø K. Int Urogynecol J 1995;6:282–291, "PFM exercise for the treatment of SUI: an exercise physiology perspective"](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/BF01901527)
- [RCT] Bø, Talseth & Holme 1999 (BMJ 318:487–93), 107 women with SUI, 6 months. The PFMT arm did **8–12 contractions 3 times a day plus a weekly group class with a skilled physiotherapist**. PFM strength gain was significantly greater after PFMT than after electrical stimulation or cones (P = 0.03). Pad-test leakage fell by **−30.2 g with PFMT vs −7.4 g with electrical stimulation** — [PubMed 10024253](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/10024253/)
- [RCT] Bø 1990 ("Effects of two different degrees of PFM exercises", Neurourol Urodyn 9:489–502): compared intensities, with weekly supervised progressive group training for 16 weeks vs a less intensive home regimen, after individual instruction with vaginal palpation. A later trial from the same group describes its home program as **"16-week home-training programme with 3 sets of 8–12 maximum PFM contractions daily"** — [Woodley Cochrane 2020 references](https://www.cochranelibrary.com/cdsr/doi/10.1002/14651858.CD007471.pub4/references); [Hay-Smith 2011 Cochrane references](https://www.cochranelibrary.com/cdsr/doi/10.1002/14651858.CD009508/references). *Conflict:* one Cochrane listing describes Bø 1990's arms as "daily home PFMT (n = 23)" vs "thrice-weekly home PFMT (n = 22)", which does not match the usual "intensive vs home" description. Check the original before quoting arm details.
- [Expert] Bø progression advice, as summarised in a 2021 review: once basic exercises are mastered, change the starting position, increase hold duration and repetitions, then add abdominal tension during situations that raise intra-abdominal pressure — [CEOG 2021;48(5) review](https://www.imrpress.com/journal/CEOG/48/5/10.31083/j.ceog4805164)

**Meta-analyses of protocol parameters**
- [MA] García-Sánchez E, Ávila-Gandía V, López-Román J, Martínez-Rodríguez A, Rubio-Arias JÁ. "What Pelvic Floor Muscle Training Load is Optimal in Minimizing Urine Loss in Women with SUI?" *Int J Environ Res Public Health* 2019;16(22):4358, doi:10.3390/ijerph16224358. **10 RCTs, 293 women**, objective pad-test outcome only. PFMT reduced urine loss *regardless of protocol*. For large effects: **program 6–12 weeks, >3 sessions/week, session length <45 min**. Subgroups: **1-h pad test SMD −1.15 (95% CI −1.86 to −0.45; 9 studies; I² = 89%)**; **24-h pad test SMD −0.53 (−0.87 to −0.19; 4 studies; I² = 0%)**. The authors state that research has not determined the most effective load — [MDPI full text](https://www.mdpi.com/1660-4601/16/22/4358); [PMC6887794](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6887794/)
- [MA] Choi H, Palmer MH, Park J. "Meta-analysis of pelvic floor muscle training: randomized controlled trials in incontinent women." *Nursing Research* 2007;56(4). Number of daily contractions and length of training were **not related to effect size, provided training included at least 24 contractions a day for at least 6 weeks**. This is a threshold, not a dose-response — [Nursing Research](https://journals.lww.com/nursingresearchonline/_layouts/15/oaks.journals/downloadpdf.aspx?an=00006199-200707000-00002); [Ovid abstract](https://www.ovid.com/jnls/nursingresearchonline/abstract/10.1097/01.nnr.0000280610.93373.e1~meta-analysis-of-pelvic-floor-muscle-training-randomized?redirectionsource=fulltextview)
- [MA + meta-regression] 2025 review of biofeedback-assisted PFMT for female SUI. Higher cure/improvement rates were associated with **mildly challenging exercises, tailoring to the patient, therapist supervision, adherence ≈80–95%, sessions <10 minutes, >1 hour of training per week, and ≥8 weeks** — [PMC12796149](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12796149)
- [Expert/secondary] A 2025 review of postmenopausal women cites supervised programs of **8–30 contractions per day for at least 3 months** as better than unsupervised regimens. This is a secondary statement — [ResearchGate 386154337](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/386154337_Effect_of_pelvic_floor_muscle_training_on_urinary_incontinence_symptoms_in_postmenopausal_women_A_systematic_review_and_meta-analysis)

**Cochrane efficacy and typical duration**
- [Cochrane] Dumoulin, Cacciari & Hay-Smith 2018 (CD005654.pub4): **31 trials, 1817 women, 14 countries**. SUI, participant-perceived cure: **RR 8.38 (95% CI 3.68–19.07)**, i.e. **505 vs 60 per 1000** (4 RCTs, 165 women, high certainty). SUI cure or improvement: **RR 6.33 (3.88–10.33); 74% vs 11%** (3 RCTs, 242 women, moderate certainty). Any UI, cure: **35% vs 6%, RR 5.34 (2.78–10.26)**. Treatment duration in these trials was **3–6 months**. Side effects were rare and minor — [Cochrane plain-language summary](https://www.cochrane.org/evidence/CD005654_pelvic-floor-muscle-training-urinary-incontinence-women); [abridged republication PMC6428911](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6428911/)
- [Cochrane] Hay-Smith et al. 2024 update (CD009508.pub2, Cochrane Database Syst Rev 2024;12:CD009508): the typical treatment duration in included trials was **12 weeks**, most often delivered by a physiotherapist. All trials confirmed a correct voluntary contraction and collected adherence data. The review includes an "exercise dose" section and a comparison of more vs fewer training days per week; those results were not retrievable here — [Cochrane 2024 full text](https://www.cochranelibrary.com/cdsr/doi/10.1002/14651858.CD009508.pub2/full); [ICS 2024 abstract #239](https://www.ics.org/2024/abstract/239)

**Hold durations and rest used in trial protocols (none tested head-to-head)**
- [RCT protocol] OAB RCT: holds of **6 s in month 1, 8 s in month 2, 10 s in month 3**, with **double-time rest** between contractions — [PMC12827014](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12827014)
- [RCT protocol] SUI trial protocol: rest **≈5 s after each contraction and 2 min between sets** at lower levels, **10 s and 3 min** at higher levels — [PMC4647572](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC4647572/)
- [RCT] **(MEN)** Milios, Ackland & Green 2019, BMC Urol 19:116, radical prostatectomy. The intensive arm did **6 sets/day of 10 fast (1 s) + 10 slow (10 s) contractions with rest equal to contraction duration = 120 contractions/day**. Usual care was **3 sets × 10 = 30/day** — [PMC6858748](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6858748/)

**Kegel's original protocol**
- [Historical] Kegel 1948 (Am J Obstet Gynecol 56:238–248) used progressive-resistance training against a pneumatic perineometer (0–100 mmHg). Patients started near **0–15 mmHg** maximal contraction and progressed toward **50–100 mmHg** — [Kegel perineometer patent and history summary](https://patents.google.com/patent/EP1032457B1/en); [Nursing Clio history of quantifying PF strength](https://nursingclio.org/2024/05/29/an-intimate-numbers-game-histories-of-quantifying-pelvic-floor-strength/); [StatPearls: Kegel Exercises](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/sites/books/NBK555898/)
- [Expert] Later reviewers stress the great variety of PFE protocols and the inconsistent use of perineometers or palpation since Kegel — [Springer: "Strengthening the pelvic floor with Kegel exercises"](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/BF00373609)

### Inferences
- Тонус's 48–114 contractions/day is **2–5× above the evidence-based minimum** (NICE 24/day; Choi threshold ≥24/day) and close to the highest-volume male RCT (Milios: 120/day). Volume is not the limiting factor. Per Choi, adding more contractions above about 24/day is unlikely to add benefit by itself.
- Holds of 3–10 s and rest ≥ hold match trial practice (6–10 s holds; rest 1–2× hold). A 6 s → 8 s → 10 s ramp over about 3 months is a published template.
- **Program length is likely too short.** At 9–21 sessions per level and 3 sessions/day, a user reaches level 8 in roughly 24–56 days (about 3.5–8 weeks, my calculation). Successful trials ran 12 weeks minimum and Bø recommends 5–6 months. Strength gains in trials were measured at 3–6 months. The top level should be treated as a long training block or a periodised cycle, not an endpoint reached in about 6 weeks.
- The strongest lever in the evidence is not dose but **(a) a correctly executed near-maximal contraction, (b) frequent feedback or supervision, and (c) adherence**. An app has to replicate these (see Q5).

### Gaps
- Full-text numbers from Bø 1990 (commonly quoted: 60% vs 17% continent/almost continent in the intensive vs home group, plus squeeze-pressure gains) and Bø 1999 (commonly quoted: 44–56% cured/"no longer a problem") could not be verified, so they are not reported as fact.
- Kegel's often-quoted daily volume (e.g. "20 min 3×/day" or "300 contractions/day") and his reported cure rate (often "84%") could not be verified from the original.
- No RCT directly compared hold durations (e.g. 6–8 s vs 10 s) or rest intervals.
- The Hay-Smith 2024 "exercise dose" and "more vs fewer days per week" results were not retrievable.

## Q2. Is daily training 3×/day better than fewer, more intense sessions? Is there dose-response data?

### Takeaway
No head-to-head RCT shows that 3 sessions a day beat fewer sessions of equal or greater intensity. García-Sánchez found large effects with more than 3 sessions a week. Choi found no dose-response above about 24 contractions a day. Bø's own strength-training framing is 3 sets of 8–12 near-maximal contractions, which she describes as either daily or 3–4 times a week. Intensity (near-maximal effort) and supervision explain more of the variance than frequency.

### Cited Findings
- [MA] Large effects were found with **>3 sessions/week**, programs of 6–12 weeks, and sessions <45 min. No upper frequency benefit was established — [García-Sánchez 2019, MDPI](https://www.mdpi.com/1660-4601/16/22/4358)
- [MA] No relation between daily contraction count and effect size **above ≥24/day for ≥6 weeks** — [Choi 2007, Nursing Research](https://journals.lww.com/nursingresearchonline/_layouts/15/oaks.journals/downloadpdf.aspx?an=00006199-200707000-00002)
- [Expert] Bø: **3 sets of 8–12 near-maximal contractions, 3–4 times per week**, for about 6 months, as the general strength principle — [Bø 2011, POGP journal](https://thepogp.co.uk/journal/21/acpwh_journal_108_spring_2011/368); [Bø 1995 IUJ](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/BF01901527). NICE instead specifies ≥8 contractions **3×/day** — [NIHR Evidence](https://evidence.nihr.ac.uk/alert/pelvic-floor-muscle-training-can-improve-symptoms-of-urinary-incontinence/)
- [MA + meta-regression] Better outcomes with **short sessions (<10 min) adding up to >1 h/week** and "mildly challenging" exercise. This supports frequent short sessions as long as each is challenging — [PMC12796149](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12796149)
- [MA] **(MEN)** Baumann et al. 2022 (Disabil Rehabil 44(19):5374–5385), **20 RCTs, 2188 men** after radical prostatectomy. PFME beat no PFME for UI remission (risk differences ≈12–25% at 3, 3–6, >6 months). Effects were "particularly evident for **higher volume, supervised** PFME in the first 6 months". **Unsupervised PFME ≈ no PFME** — [UroToday abstract](https://urotoday.com/recent-abstracts/pelvic-health-reconstruction/post-prostatectomy-incontinence/132408-supervised-pelvic-floor-muscle-exercise-is-more-effective-than-unsupervised-pelvic-floor-muscle-exercise-at-improving-urinary-incontinence-in-prostate-cancer-patients-following-radical-prostatectomy-a-systematic-review-and-meta-analysis.html); [PubMed 34550846](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34550846/)
- [RCT] **(MEN)** Milios 2019: 97 men randomised to high-volume (120/day, fast + slow) vs low-volume (30/day) training, starting 5 weeks before surgery and continuing 12 weeks after. The high-volume program improved post-surgical PFM function, reduced post-prostatectomy incontinence and improved incontinence-related QoL — [BMC Urol 2019;19:116](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s12894-019-0546-5). A 2025 SR notes that more intensive, prolonged protocols such as Milios's tend to report better recovery — [BMC Urol 2025 SR](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s12894-025-01932-2)
- [SR] **(MEN)** 2024 scoping review of 24 post-prostatectomy studies: **no consensus** on start, duration, contraction/relaxation time or intensity. The most frequent prescription was **3 sessions/day, 6–15 contractions/session** — [Rev Latino-Am Enfermagem scoping review](https://revistas.usp.br/rlae/en/article/view/230887)
- [Guideline, general exercise science] ACSM 2026 umbrella review (137 SRs, >30,000 participants; Med Sci Sports Exerc, April 2026): the biggest gain comes from doing *any* resistance training vs none. Train each muscle group **≥2 days/week**. Loads **≥~80% 1RM** give the most robust maximal-strength gains. Complex periodisation showed no clear benefit. Individualise for adherence — [ACSM news release](https://acsm.org/science-spotlight-acsm-releases-new-position-stand-on-resistance-training/); [ACSM infographic](https://acsm.org/wp-content/uploads/2026/03/Resistance-Training-Position-Stand-infographic.pdf); [Newswise](https://www.newswise.com/articles/acsm-unveils-landmark-2026-resistance-training-guidelines-first-update-in-17-years)

### Inferences
- For the anal sphincter / PFM (small, fatigue-resistant, mostly type I muscles; see Q4), daily or near-daily training is safe and standard. The evidence does not require 3 sessions a day. **Two possible configurations:** (a) keep 3 short sessions a day but make at least one a high-intensity "strength" session with maximal effort, with the others lighter (motor control, endurance); or (b) 1–2 sessions a day at higher effort. Both meet NICE's minimum and Choi's threshold.
- For adherence, a single required daily session plus optional extras may be better than three mandatory sessions. Long-term adherence is the main determinant of maintained results (Q5, Q6).
- Volume in the "high" range (Milios 120/day) was tolerated and effective in men after surgery. For a healthy man, 48–114/day is not excessive. But no evidence shows it beats about 30–40 high-quality contractions a day.

### Gaps
- No RCT compared 3×/day vs 1×/day vs 3×/week at matched or higher intensity in PFMT.
- No study defines PFM "%MVC" loads equivalent to ACSM's %1RM. In PFMT practice, "near-maximal" is self-rated or measured by manometry or EMG.

## Q3. How do ACSM strength-training principles translate to PFMT, and what is the neural vs hypertrophy timeline?

### Takeaway
Bø has applied exercise-science principles to PFMT since 1995: specificity, overload (near-maximal contractions), progression, enough duration (≥3–6 months) and maintenance. Early strength gains are mostly neural. Hypertrophy follows over months. The specific claim "hypertrophy after about 5 months" for the PFM could not be verified from a primary source; it is an extrapolation from general skeletal-muscle physiology.

### Cited Findings
- [Expert] The scientific basis of PFMT is exercise physiology. Efficacy depends on exposing the isolated muscle to **sufficient load intensity, duration and frequency**, plus adherence to maintenance — [Bø, POGP journal](https://thepogp.co.uk/journal/21/acpwh_journal_108_spring_2011/368) (summary quote via search of Bø's POGP article and ICS workshop materials)
- [Expert] Bø's exercise-physiology review: 8–12 contractions × 3 series, 3–4×/week, ≥15–20 weeks. Strength gains measured after 3–6 months. A proposed mechanism is **more effective automatic motor-unit firing (neural adaptation)** — [Bø 1995 IUJ](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/BF01901527); [ICS abstract on PFMT for POP](https://www.ics.org/Abstracts/Publish/105/000166.pdf)
- [Phys, general muscle] Neural factors accounted for most strength improvement during the **first 4 weeks** of an 8-week resistance program. Significant fibre hypertrophy was detected **only after about 6 weeks** (Staron). Hypertrophic responses with prolonged training are finite, within about 12 months — [ScienceDirect topic overview: neural adaptation](https://www.sciencedirect.com/topics/medicine-and-dentistry/neural-adaptation); [ResearchGate figure: neural vs hypertrophy contribution over time](https://www.researchgate.net/figure/The-contribution-of-neural-adaptation-and-muscle-hypertrophy-to-strength-gains-changes_fig1_268258974)
- [Guideline, general] ACSM 2009: novices **8–12 RM**. Frequency **2–3 d/week (novice), 3–4 (intermediate), 4–5 (advanced)**. **Increase load 2–10% when 1–2 reps above the target can be done** — [ACSM 2009 Position Stand PDF](https://www.sportgeneeskunde.com/wp-content/uploads/ACSM-Position-Stand-Progression-Models-in-Resistance-Training-for-Healthy-Adults.pdf); [SFU copy](https://sfu.ca/~ryand/kin343/ACSMresistance.pdf). It has been superseded by the 2026 umbrella review (Q2) — [ACSM 2026](https://acsm.org/science-spotlight-acsm-releases-new-position-stand-on-resistance-training/)
- [RCT] Braekken, Majida, Engh & Bø 2010 (Am J Obstet Gynecol 203(2):170.e1–7), assessor-blinded RCT in prolapse with a 6-month PFMT program. PFMT significantly **increased PFM strength and thickness, lifted the bladder neck and rectal ampulla and narrowed the levator hiatus area**. This is morphological evidence of structural, hypertrophy-like change after about 6 months — [summary in later review](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s00192-012-1973-y); [Bø POGP 2011](https://thepogp.co.uk/journal/21/acpwh_journal_108_spring_2011/368); [ClinicalTrials NCT00271297](https://clinicaltrials.gov/study/NCT00271297)
- [Pilot] **(MEN)** Labun et al. 2026 (Front Public Health, doi 10.3389/fpubh.2026.1781361): **16 healthy men aged 23 ± 4**, 6 weeks of self-performed **5–10-min isolated PFM sessions added to their regular strength training**, measured by anal-probe sEMG (Glazer protocol). **MVC EMG +52% (p = 0.002; r = 0.78)**. Mean amplitude of five 3-s quick contractions rose **7.94 → 12.72 µV (p = 0.006; r = 0.68)**. All contraction types improved. No control group. An EMG rise at 6 weeks is consistent with neural adaptation — [PMC13106149](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13106149/); [Frontiers full text](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/public-health/articles/10.3389/fpubh.2026.1781361/full)
- [Cochrane] Hay-Smith 2024: **co-ordinated training** (body movement with a concurrent PFM contraction) vs direct PFMT may slightly improve QoL (SMD −0.22, 95% CI −0.44 to −0.01; 8 trials, 356 women; low certainty). Direct PFMT beats indirect training such as general exercise without PFM contraction (SMD 0.70, 0.38–1.02; 4 trials; low certainty). Adding indirect training to direct PFMT makes little difference (SMD −0.08). Resistance devices are very uncertain (SMD 0.22, −0.04 to 0.48). This supports **specificity**: the PFM must be contracted deliberately — [Cochrane CD009508.pub2](https://www.cochranelibrary.com/cdsr/doi/10.1002/14651858.CD009508.pub2/full)

### Inferences
Mapping to Тонус:
- **Specificity:** isolated, deliberate contractions are needed. General fitness or core work does not substitute (Hay-Smith 2024). The app already isolates. Adding "co-ordinated" functional contractions in later levels (contract during movement, lifting, coughing) is supported by low-certainty evidence.
- **Overload:** the "slow maximal holds" block is the overload stimulus. Gains depend on genuinely near-maximal effort, so cue "maximal squeeze, then hold" rather than just "hold for N s". Progressing only hold time (3 → 10 s) mainly trains endurance. Strength overload needs effort near the maximum.
- **Progression:** ACSM's rule (raise load once the target is exceeded by 1–2 reps) maps onto: advance a level when the user completes all reps at the target hold *with full intensity*, rated "not hard". The app's "not hard" rule is a reasonable analogue, but with only 9–21 sessions per level it progresses faster than the neural (about 4–8 weeks) and structural (months) timelines.
- **Expected timeline to communicate to users:** about 4–8 weeks for noticeable neural or control gains (Labun pilot: +52% EMG at 6 weeks); 3–6 months for structural or strength gains (Braekken: thickness and hiatus changes at 6 months).

### Gaps
- No primary PFM source was found confirming "hypertrophy after about 5 months". It is cited in some PFMT teaching material but not verified here (no DiNubile source found). Treat it as expert extrapolation.
- Exact Braekken 2010 percentages (thickness, hiatus area, strength) were not retrievable.
- No male anal-sphincter hypertrophy study was found.

## Q4. Fast-twitch (quick flicks, power) vs slow holds vs submaximal endurance vs "elevator": is any superior, does combining help, and what is the EAS fibre composition?

### Takeaway
No RCT shows one contraction type is superior. Successful protocols nearly always combine maximal holds with fast contractions (Bø's 1999 protocol and Milios's male protocol both combined slow holds with quick 1-s contractions). The adult human external anal sphincter is mostly type I (slow) fibre. The type I share falls after age 70. The functional "knack" (pre-contracting before a pressure rise) has strong immediate effects. No trial evidence exists for graded "elevator" exercises.

### Cited Findings
**Fibre type**
- [Phys, human histology] EAS fibre types across age: **type II predominated in fetuses (26–40 wk); type I became dominant by childhood and persisted in adults aged 56–70**. After 70, type I fibres diminished, giving a **slow:fast ratio of 3:2 at age 78–81** — [Lierse et al., Eur J Pediatr Surg 1993;3(1):28–32, PubMed 8466872](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/8466872/); [Thieme PDF](https://www.thieme-connect.de/products/ejournals/pdf/10.1055/s-2008-1063503.pdf)
- [Phys, human] In **24 men with haemorrhoids**, the EAS showed **92% type I** predominance, markedly greater than controls (control values not available in the snippet) — [Gut 1981;22(1):45](https://gut.bmj.com/content/22/1/45)
- [Phys] The EAS "consists mostly of slow twitch fibers that allow extended continuous contraction" (tertiary source) — [Wikipedia: External anal sphincter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/External_anal_sphincter)
- [Phys, animal, do NOT extrapolate] Rabbit EAS is about 0.2% type 1, 62.5% type 2A, 37.3% type 2B, and rat anal sphincter about 5% type 1. Rodent and lagomorph sphincters are fast-twitch, unlike humans — [Buffini 2010 J Anat (rat)](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/j.1469-7580.2010.01263.x); [Med Sci Monit](https://medscimonit.com/abstract/full/idArt/884037)

**Fatigue behaviour of the EAS**
- [Phys] Women with faecal incontinence had **less fatigable** EAS than continent women, and EAS fatigability decreased with age. The authors hypothesise more slow-twitch fibres (a weaker but fatigue-resistant sphincter) — [PubMed 22122610 "Fatigue rate of the external anal sphincter"](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/22122610/)
- [Phys] With a strength-training resistance device, **9/12 subjects (75%)** showed neuromuscular fatigue, appearing after about **half of 40 repetitive short anal squeezes** (about 20). The authors conclude that **load must be increased to reach neuromuscular fatigue** in anal rehab exercise — [Am J Physiol GI 2020, doi 10.1152/ajpgi.00456.2020](https://journals.physiology.org/doi/full/10.1152/ajpgi.00456.2020)
- [Phys] An EMG and manometry fatigue protocol (19 patients) showed a significant drop in RMS between the first and last MVC — [J Neurogastroenterol Motil 2020, PMC7786088](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7786088/)
- [Phys] In 131 patients, the manometric fatigue rate and fatigue rate index were not useful in routine colorectal practice — [PubMed 16328124](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16328124)

**Contraction-type evidence**
- [RCT] **(MEN)** Milios 2019 targeted **both fast- and slow-twitch fibres** (10 × 1-s fast + 10 × 10-s slow per set, rest equal to work, 6 sets a day) and outperformed low-volume training — [PMC6858748](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6858748/)
- [Expert/RCT protocol] **(MEN)** Hodges group (MaTchUP protocol, BMJ Open 2019;9:e028288): male continence depends on urethral smooth muscle plus three striated muscles (**striated urethral sphincter, puborectalis, bulbocavernosus**). Brief strong "standard" contractions may not target the muscles that control continence, so the authors argue for muscle-specific, individualised training — [BMJ Open protocol PDF](https://rcastoragev2.blob.core.windows.net/8b5985e5c664b4de1d23069fc897e53a/PMC6502040.pdf); review: Hall LM, Aljuraifani R, Hodges PW. *Neurourol Urodyn* 2018;37(7):2053–, "Design of programs to train pelvic floor muscles in men with urinary dysfunction: systematic review" — [MaTchUP protocol on ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/332886525_Efficacy_of_a_personalised_pelvic_floor_muscle_training_programme_on_urinary_incontinence_after_radical_prostatectomy_MaTchUP_Protocol_for_a_randomised_controlled_trial)
- [RCT, within-subject] The **"Knack"**: Miller, Ashton-Miller & DeLancey 1998 (J Am Geriatr Soc), 27 women aged about 68 with mild-moderate SUI. After 1 week, a voluntary PFM pre-contraction about 1 s before coughing reduced leakage by **98.2% (medium cough, P = .009)** and **73.3% (deep cough, P = .003)**. The reduction did **not** correlate with digital PFM strength, so timing and skill matter as well as strength — [PubMed 9670874](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/9670874/)
- [Cochrane] Hay-Smith 2011/2024 compared "type of contraction" approaches but found low- to moderate-certainty evidence that some approaches beat others and others make little difference. Co-ordinated (functional) training gave a slight QoL benefit — [Cochrane 2024](https://www.cochranelibrary.com/cdsr/doi/10.1002/14651858.CD009508.pub2/full); [abridged 2011, PubMed 23183454](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23183454/)
- [Pilot] **(MEN)** In healthy young men, both MVC and quick-contraction EMG amplitude rose over 6 weeks of mixed training — [PMC13106149](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13106149/)

### Inferences
- Тонус's structure (maximal holds + quick 1-s contractions + long submaximal holds + relaxation) matches the multi-component designs used in successful trials (Bø 1999; Milios 2019). Keep the combination.
- The EAS in a healthy adult man is predominantly type I. This supports endurance holds and long submaximal "tonic" holds (20–30 s at ~50%) for "tone". Fast contractions matter for reflexive closure during sudden pressure rises (cough, sprint, lift), per the knack data.
- **Add a functional "knack" drill** (pre-contract → cough, sneeze, squat or lift) in later levels. The effect is immediate, large and skill-based, and it maps directly onto the "well-controlled" goal.
- The "elevator" (graded contraction) is a motor-control or proprioception drill with plausible rationale but no outcome evidence. Treat it as an optional coordination exercise, not a core dose component.
- Fatigue data (onset after about 20 repeated short squeezes; Am J Physiol GI 2020) suggest the app's 8–20 quick contractions per set sit just below or at fatigue onset. That is acceptable. Pushing quick sets above about 20 reps likely adds fatigue without benefit; increase effort instead.

### Gaps
- No exact adult human EAS type I percentage (e.g. a muscle-biopsy figure for healthy young or middle-aged men) was found. Puborectalis fibre composition was not found.
- No RCT compared fast-only vs slow-only vs combined PFMT outcomes.
- No evidence was found on "elevator"/graded exercises.

## Q5. Supervised vs unsupervised, group vs individual, diaries and app reminders, dropout, and how adherence affects results

### Takeaway
Supervision is the most consistent effect modifier. Women with frequent professional contact improve far more often than those with little or none. In men after prostatectomy, unsupervised PFMT performed about the same as no training. Group training is non-inferior to individual training. Apps such as Tät produce clinically meaningful symptom improvements versus waitlist, but only about 41% of users trained daily. Long-term adherence ranges from 10% to 70%.

### Cited Findings
- [Cochrane] Hay-Smith 2011 (21 trials, 1490 women, 11 comparisons). With more professional contact (weekly or twice-weekly group supervision plus individual appointments), **10% reported no improvement vs 43%** with individual appointments only (**RR for no improvement 0.29, 95% CI 0.15–0.55; 4 trials**). Women with regular (e.g. weekly) supervision were more likely to report improvement than those with little or no supervision. Conclusion: offer "reasonably frequent" professional contact — [PubMed 23183454](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23183454/); [Cochrane CD009508](https://www.cochranelibrary.com/cdsr/doi/10.1002/14651858.CD009508/references)
- [MA] **(MEN)** Baumann 2022: **unsupervised PFME ≈ no PFME** after prostatectomy. Supervised, higher-volume PFME was effective. Biofeedback helped only in the first 3 months — [PubMed 34550846](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34550846/)
- [RCT] Dumoulin et al. 2020 GROUP trial (JAMA Intern Med), **362 older women** with stress or mixed UI: group-based PFMT was **non-inferior** to individual PFMT for percentage reduction in leakage episodes at 1 year (difference 4%; 95% CI −10% to 7%; margin 10%). **88% (319) completed 1-year follow-up** — [JAMA Intern Med](https://jamanetwork.com/journals/jamainternalmedicine/fullarticle/2768776); [PubMed 32744599](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32744599/); [PEDro synopsis](https://pedro.org.au/english/group-based-pelvic-floor-muscle-training-is-not-inferior-to-individual-training-for-urinary-incontinence-in-older-women/)
- [MA] Group vs individual or home PFMT systematic review — [Int Urogynecol J 2017, doi 10.1007/s00192-016-3133-2](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s00192-016-3133-2) (numbers not extracted)
- [RCT] Asklund et al. 2017 (Neurourol Urodyn), Tät app vs postponed treatment, women with SUI, outcomes at 3 months. **ICIQ-UI SF reduced 3.9 vs 0.9 points (p < 0.001); ICIQ-LUTSqol 4.8 vs 0.7 (p = 0.005)**. Only **41% (25/61) of app users trained daily** (logged in-app) — [Wiley](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1002/nau.23116); [Cochrane 2018 PMC6516955](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6516955/)
- [Cohort/FU] Hoffman 2017, 2-year follow-up of the Tät app group: mean ICIQ-UI SF decrease **3.1**, ICIQ-LUTSqol **4.0**. Most of the effect was retained — [ResearchGate: 2-year follow-up](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/318497305_Self-management_of_stress_urinary_incontinence_via_a_mobile_app_2-year_follow-up_of_a_randomized_controlled_trial)
- [MA] App-based PFMT for female UI, meta-analysis of RCTs — [PMC13531428](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13531428/). Online PFMT across life stages — [Wang 2026 Int J Nurs Pract](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/ijn.70177). Digital PF interventions in pregnancy and postpartum, adherence determinants — [Int Urogynecol J 2026, PubMed 42234137](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42234137/) (pooled numbers not extracted)
- [MA] Villa-Del-Pino et al. 2025 (Life 15(4):613), compliance and adherence to PF exercise therapy: 7 studies, 2190 participants. **42% of studies reported adherence >80%**. Short-term symptom severity was not statistically different between groups. QoL improved significantly — [PMC12028920](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12028920/)
- [MA + meta-regression] Better outcomes with **adherence ≈80–95%** and therapist supervision — [PMC12796149](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12796149)
- [SR] Long-term PFMT adherence **10%–70%** (19 studies, 1141 women, 1–15 years) — [Bø & Hilde 2013, Neurourol Urodyn "Does it work in the long term?"](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1002/nau.22292)
- [Cochrane] Hay-Smith 2024: all included trials confirmed a correct voluntary contraction before training and collected adherence data — [Cochrane 2024](https://www.cochranelibrary.com/cdsr/doi/10.1002/14651858.CD009508.pub2/full)
- [Cochrane, anal] **Faecal incontinence:** Norton & Cody (CD002111), 21 RCTs. Biofeedback + exercises beat exercises alone in one small trial (**RR for failing to achieve full continence 0.70, 95% CI 0.52–0.94**). Biofeedback + electrical stimulation beat ES alone (**RR 0.47, 0.33–0.65**). "Standards of treatment are still lacking." For patients who failed simple measures, biofeedback beats exercises alone — [Cochrane plain-language summary](https://www.cochrane.org/CD002111/INCONT_exercises-of-the-muscles-around-the-anus-with-or-without-biofeedback-aids-for-knowing-when-the-muscles-are-contracting-for-the-treatment-of-faecal-incontinence-in-adults); [PubMed 16855987](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16855987/); [PMC6830410 "What does Cochrane say…"](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6830410/)
- [RCT, anal] Norton et al. 2003 (Gastroenterology), **171 FI patients, 4 arms** (standard care/advice, + sphincter exercises, + clinic biofeedback, + home EMG biofeedback). Patients improved **resting, squeeze and sustained-squeeze pressures (all P < 0.002)**, largely maintained at 1 year — [Gastroenterology 2003](https://www.gastrojournal.org/article/S0016-5085(03)01368-4/fulltext)
- [Pilot, anal] Biofeedback-based anal strengthening after radiotherapy: median squeeze-pressure improvement **15 mmHg (range −16 to +100)**. Endurance (fatigue time) improved only modestly — [PMC7707912](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC7707912/)

### Inferences
- An app cannot palpate, so it cannot confirm a correct contraction. That is the main risk for an unsupervised consumer program, given NICE's warning about incorrect technique and Baumann's "unsupervised ≈ none" finding in men. Mitigations: rigorous onboarding self-checks (e.g. "stop-the-flow" as a one-off test only, not a training method; the anal "lift and squeeze" cue; feeling for the perineal lift with a finger; mirror check); a prompt to see a pelvic-health physio if no lift is felt; optional integration with consumer EMG or pressure biofeedback devices.
- The app can replicate the elements of supervision that drive benefit: **frequent contact or feedback** (weekly check-ins, re-testing max-hold and quick-reps capacity), a **training diary** (already implicit in completion tracking), and **reminders**. The Tät data show that self-guided apps work, but only about 40% of users train daily, so design for imperfect adherence: make one session/day the "core" and the others optional.
- For a healthy man with no dysfunction, cure-rate data do not apply directly. The relevant targets are strength, endurance and control, which can be tracked in-app via self-tests (max hold time at full squeeze, number of quick contractions before fade).

### Gaps
- Pooled dropout rates for app-based or unsupervised PFMT were not extracted.
- No app RCT in healthy men was found. No evidence was found on anal-sphincter-specific app training.
- No direct evidence was found that reminders by themselves (vs no reminders) improve outcomes.

## Q6. Maintenance: how much training preserves gains, and what does long-term follow-up show?

### Takeaway
Gains partly persist. Five years after a 6-month intensive program, 70% of women still trained at least once a week and strength was maintained, although pad-test leakage had worsened. Long-term reviews show that adherence falls (10–70%) and surgery rates vary widely (4.9–58%). About once a week of continued training appears to be the practical maintenance dose in Bø's cohorts. This is observational evidence, not trial evidence.

### Cited Findings
- [Cohort/FU] Bø & Talseth 1996 (Obstet Gynecol 87(2):261–5): all **23 women** from a 6-month intensive PFMT regimen were followed **5 years** after organised training ended. **3/23 had surgery. 16 (70%) still exercised the PFM ≥1×/week. PFM strength was maintained.** Leakage on pad test and leakage index increased significantly, but **75% had no leakage on stress test and 70% were satisfied** — [PubMed 8559536](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/8559536/); [Green Journal](https://journals.lww.com/greenjournal/abstract/00006250-199602000-00021~long-term-effect-of-pelvic-floor-muscle-exercise-5-years)
- [Cohort/FU] Bø, Kvarstein & Nygaard 2005, "Lower urinary tract symptoms and PFM exercise adherence after 15 years", Obstet Gynecol 105(5 Pt 1):999–1005: a 15-year follow-up of the 1990 RCT, with subjective cure or improvement measured at 6 months, 5 years and 15 years — [Cochrane reference listing](https://www.cochranelibrary.com/cdsr/doi/10.1002/14651858.CD009508.pub2/references/zh); [Bø 2013 SR](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1002/nau.22292)
- [SR] Bø & Hilde 2013: 19 studies, 1141 women followed 1–15 years. **Long-term adherence 10–70%. Surgery rates 4.9–58%** — [Neurourol Urodyn 2013](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1002/nau.22292)
- [Cohort/FU] Beyar 2017: 208 women; only **8 (3.8%)** had SUI surgery within 5 years after the training program — [Neurourol Urodyn 2017](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/abs/10.1002/nau.22888)
- [Cohort/FU] Ten-year follow-up after conservative SUI treatment — [Int Urogynecol J 2008, doi 10.1007/s00192-007-0550-2](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s00192-007-0550-2). Twelve-year follow-up of PFMT vs mid-urethral sling: no significant ITT differences — [PMC11969909](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC11969909/)
- [Cohort/FU] Tät app: symptom gains largely persisted at 2 years (ICIQ-UI SF −3.1) — [ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/318497305_Self-management_of_stress_urinary_incontinence_via_a_mobile_app_2-year_follow-up_of_a_randomized_controlled_trial)
- [Guideline] NICE: continue PFMT beyond 3 months if it is beneficial — [NIHR Evidence](https://evidence.nihr.ac.uk/alert/pelvic-floor-muscle-training-can-improve-symptoms-of-urinary-incontinence/)
- [Expert] Bø lists **adherence to maintenance protocols** as one of the four determinants of PFMT efficacy — [Bø POGP 2011](https://thepogp.co.uk/journal/21/acpwh_journal_108_spring_2011/368)

### Inferences
- Add an explicit **maintenance mode** after the top level: e.g. 1 full-intensity session 2–3×/week (ACSM 2026 says ≥2 d/week preserves and builds strength), or a minimum of 1 session/week (Bø's 70% "≥ weekly" cohort maintained strength), plus daily functional "knack" use.
- Expect reversibility: symptoms or leakage drifted upward over 5 years even while strength was maintained. Periodic "re-test and refresh" blocks (e.g. a 4-week intensive block every 3–6 months) are a reasonable, evidence-informed but untested design.

### Gaps
- The 15-year figures from Bø 2005 (commonly quoted: about 50% had SUI surgery in both groups and about 28% still trained ≥ weekly) could not be verified, so they are not reported as fact.
- No trial randomised maintenance doses (e.g. 1×/week vs daily).
- No long-term male data were found outside the post-prostatectomy setting.

## Q7. Overtraining and fatigue of the pelvic floor; rest between contractions and between sessions

### Takeaway
No evidence was found that PFMT at trial doses causes harm or overtraining. Cochrane reports rare, minor side effects. Rest intervals in trials are design choices, not tested variables: typically rest equal to or double the hold within a set, and 1–3 minutes between sets. EAS fatigue appears after about 20 repeated short squeezes.

### Cited Findings
- [Cochrane] Negative side effects of PFMT were rare. In the two trials that reported them, they were minor — [Cochrane CD005654 summary](https://www.cochrane.org/evidence/CD005654_pelvic-floor-muscle-training-urinary-incontinence-women)
- [RCT protocol] Rest = 2× hold time (6/8/10 s holds) — [PMC12827014](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12827014)
- [RCT protocol] Rest 5 s per contraction and 2 min between sets (lower level), or 10 s and 3 min (higher level) — [PMC4647572](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC4647572/)
- [RCT] **(MEN)** Rest = contraction duration, 120 contractions/day, effective and tolerated — [PMC6858748](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6858748/)
- [Phys] EAS fatigue appeared after about half of 40 repetitive short squeezes in 75% of subjects. To reach neuromuscular fatigue (a training stimulus), working load needs to be increased — [Am J Physiol GI 2020](https://journals.physiology.org/doi/full/10.1152/ajpgi.00456.2020)
- [RCT] A 2023 Int Urogynecol J trial examined whether multiple-component *intensive* PFMT reduces PFM fatigue and symptoms in women with UI. Details not extracted — [Springer, doi 10.1007/s00192-023-05499-0](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s00192-023-05499-0)
- [Expert, secondary] Doing the knack "too soon" may fatigue the muscles and reduce power. This is clinical commentary — [Pelvic Pain Rehab blog](https://pelvicpainrehab.com/blog/what-is-the-knack-and-why-does-it-work-to-prevent-urine-leakage/)

### Inferences
- Тонус's rest ≥ hold for maximal holds and 2 s off after 1-s quick contractions (rest 2× work) match trial practice. For maximal holds at higher levels (10 s), consider **rest of 1–2× hold (10–20 s)** to keep each rep near-maximal. Fatigued submaximal reps dilute the overload stimulus.
- Insert short inter-block pauses (about 30–60 s) between the maximal-hold block and the quick-contraction block, mirroring the 1–3 min between sets in trials, scaled to small muscle mass. This is an extrapolation.
- Overtraining risk appears negligible at ≤120 contractions/day. More relevant risks: (a) chronic hypertonicity or poor relaxation in some men (pelvic pain); the app's relaxation and "reverse Kegel" block is sensible; (b) substitution with glutes, adductors or abdominals and breath-holding.

### Gaps
- No RCT varied rest intervals or between-session recovery (e.g. 3×/day vs 1×/day) and measured PFM fatigue or outcomes.
- No studies were found on PFM overtraining or hypertonicity caused by excessive Kegels. Clinical reports exist but none were retrieved here.

## Q8. "Advanced" progression methods: positions, functional loading (co-contraction while lifting), longer programs, increasing intensity

### Takeaway
Position progression (lying → sitting → standing) is common practice but showed no outcome advantage in the one RCT. Functional or co-ordinated training has low-certainty evidence of a slight extra benefit. The knack is a high-yield functional skill. Resistance devices have very uncertain added value. Longer programs (≥12 weeks to 6 months) and supervised high volume or intensity have the most consistent support.

### Cited Findings
- [RCT] Borello-France et al. 2006 (Phys Ther 86(7):974–986), 44 women with SUI, **supine-only vs supine + upright** PFMT: **position did not affect outcomes**. Pooled, both improved significantly in bladder diary, IIQ, PFM strength and urodynamics. PEDro score 5/10 — [PEDro record 15668](https://search.pedro.org.au/search-results/record-detail/15668)
- [Phys] Lower-limb position and peri-hip co-contraction affect the efficiency of voluntary PFM contraction (2025). Supine kneeling gave greater bladder-base elevation than standard supine — [PMC12113777](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC12113777/)
- [Cochrane] Co-ordinated (functional) training vs direct PFMT: slight QoL benefit, SMD −0.22 (low certainty). Resistance device vs none: very uncertain — [Cochrane 2024](https://www.cochranelibrary.com/cdsr/doi/10.1002/14651858.CD009508.pub2/full)
- [RCT] Knack (pre-contraction before cough): −98.2% / −73.3% leakage at 1 week — [PubMed 9670874](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/9670874/)
- [Expert] Bø's progression sequence: vary starting position → longer holds → more reps → add abdominal tension during raised intra-abdominal pressure — [CEOG 2021 review](https://www.imrpress.com/journal/CEOG/48/5/10.31083/j.ceog4805164)
- [Pilot] **(MEN)** Labun 2026: PFM sessions integrated into regular strength-training routines (5–10 min) produced +52% MVC EMG in 6 weeks in healthy young men — [PMC13106149](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13106149/)
- [RCT] PFMT in female functional-fitness (CrossFit-type) exercisers, assessor-blinded RCT — [PMC11103308](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11103308/). PFMT in power- and weightlifters with SUI, pilot — [PMC11245411](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11245411/) (results not extracted)
- [Guideline, general] ACSM 2026: intensity ≥~80% 1RM drives maximal strength. Complex periodisation is not clearly beneficial. Individualise for adherence — [ACSM 2026](https://acsm.org/science-spotlight-acsm-releases-new-position-stand-on-resistance-training/)
- [Cochrane, anal] Biofeedback added to sphincter exercises improved full-continence odds (RR for failure 0.70). Feedback is a form of intensity or quality progression — [Cochrane CD002111](https://www.cochrane.org/CD002111/INCONT_exercises-of-the-muscles-around-the-anus-with-or-without-biofeedback-aids-for-knowing-when-the-muscles-are-contracting-for-the-treatment-of-faecal-incontinence-in-adults)

### Inferences
Possible progression ladder for Тонус's upper levels (evidence-informed, not trial-tested):
1. **Intensity first:** cue "maximal effort" holds; add "pulse-on-top" (3–4 quick contractions at the end of a maximal hold, a feature commonly described in Bø-type protocols; see Gaps).
2. **Position:** sitting → standing → **functional positions** (squat hold, lunge, single-leg stance, walking). Position alone did not change outcomes (Borello-France), so treat it as specificity or transfer for an athletic user rather than a dose increase.
3. **Functional loading / co-ordination:** contract before and during **lifting, jumping, coughing, sneezing**. Practise the knack at 1 s before the effort; Bø's "add abdominal tension during raised IAP".
4. **Duration:** extend the program to **≥12 weeks of progression plus open-ended maintenance**, in line with 3–6-month trials.
5. **Feedback:** optional biofeedback (consumer EMG or pressure devices) is the "advanced" tool with the best anal-sphincter evidence (Norton & Cody).
6. Weighted vaginal cones and resistance devices have no male analogue and their added value is very uncertain (Cochrane 2024). Not recommended as progression.

### Gaps
- No RCT tested a structured multi-level progression scheme vs a fixed program.
- No trials were found on anal-sphincter co-contraction during heavy lifting in healthy men. The weightlifter and functional-fitness trials are in women and their numbers were not extracted.
- The specific Bø 1999 detail "6–8 s hold + 3–4 fast contractions on top" could not be verified in this session (the search tool flagged it as unconfirmed).
